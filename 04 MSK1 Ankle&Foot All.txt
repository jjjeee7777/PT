	Ottawa Ankle Rules: Bachmann, BMJ (2003)
		Error in current practice:
			Most patients who go the emergency room with an ankle sprain injury will receive a radiograph
			However, only 15% have a fracture
		Why use the Ottawa Ankle Rules?:
			A clinical decision tool to avoid unnecessary radiographs
		Physical Therapy and Direct Access:
			May be presented with situation soon after injury with no screening from physician
			It will be your responsibility to decide to treat or refer out for further evaluation
	Bachmann, BMJ (2003): ("Ottawa Ankle Rules.png")
		-Systematic review with 32 studies meeting inclusion criteria of systematic review
		-27 studies were used for meta-analysis
		-Found negative likelihood ratio for ankle and midfoot of 0.08
		-Using – LR, probability of ankle fracture < 1.4% when applying rule
		-Accurate instrument for excluding fractures of ankle and mid-foot
			Sensitivity – nearly 100% (negative findings rule out ankle fracture)
			Specificity – median value = 31.5 (positive findings may be false)
			Application of tool – reduce unnecessary radiographs by 30-40%
		-----------------------------------------------------------------
		-Less than 2% of patients who were negative for fracture actually had a fracture
		-More interested in identifying that the test is positive, given the pathology is present (Sensitivity) (few false negatives)
		-Less interested in identifying the test is negative, given the pathology is absent (Specificity) (few false positives)
		-----------------------------------------------------------------

	Heel Pain / Plantar Fasciitis: Γ('Heel') McPoil T et al, JOSPT  (2008)
		Content experts as appointed by the Ortho section of APTA
			Performed systematic review and review of evidence
			Present the strength of the reviewed evidence for different interventions of heel pain
		Risk Factors:
			Limited ankle DF ROM 
			High BMI in non-athletic populations
			Moderate evidence
		Clinical course of pathology:
			Some moderate evidence to suggest 80% will have resolution of symptoms within a 12-month period
			Key feature is pain with initial steps after period of inactivity or non-weight bearing
		Interventions:
			Modalities:
				Dexamethasone 0.4% or acetic acid 5% via iontophoresis
				Short-term pain relief
				Moderate evidence
			Manual Therapy:
				TC posterior glide, subtalar lateral glide Γ('subtalar'), first MTP mobilization Γ('MTP'), subtalar joint distraction
				Currently only minimal evidence to support the use of such procedures
			Stretching:
				Calf muscle and plantar fascia-specific technique
				Can provide short term relief (2-4 months)
				Need to perform at least 2x per day
				Moderate evidence
			Taping:
				Low-Dye or calcaneal taping can provide short term pain relief
				Weak evidence
			Orthotics:
				Short term pain relief (3 months) and improved function
				No real difference between custom made or OTC orthotics 
				No evidence for prolonged use of greater one year
				Strong evidence
			Night splints:
				When symptom duration is greater than 6 months, night splints should be considered

		Thrust and Nonthrust mobilization: Whitman et al, JOSPT (2009)
			Design:
				Prospective-Cohort study to develop a CPR to ID patients s/p inversion ankle sprain who would benefit from manual therapy and exercise
			Methods:
				Consecutive patients s/p inversion ankle injury (n=85)
				Performed standardized exam
				Given manual therapy and general mobility exercise (applied treatment to everybody)
				Patients classified as having successful outcome at 2nd or 3rd based on self reported perceived recovery
			Success vs. Non-success:
				Based on treatment response on the Global Rating of Change (GROC)
				Success = +5 to +7
				Nonsuccess = -7 to +4
				Examined differences in mean values of self-report measures of success vs. nonsuccess groups via t-tests
			What are the variables?
				Symptoms worse when standing
				Symptoms worse in evening
				Navicular drop > 5.5 mm
				Distal tibiofibular joint hypomobility
			Results:
				64/85 (75%) had a successful outcome (pretest probability)
				Logistic regression determined CPR with 4 variables
				If 3 of 4 variables present – 
					+ LR = 5.9
					Posttest probability = 95% (pretest probability 75%)
			Conclusions:
				Application of the CPR allows increases the likelihood of successful treatment to 95%
				Treatment success can result in a dramatic improvement in status
			Mobilizations performed: ("Thrust and Nonthrust mobilization.png")
				Lateral glide and eversion mobilization
				Proximal tibiofibular joint thrust mobilization
				Distal tibiofibular joint A-P mobilization
				Talocrural joint distraction thrust mobilization
				Talocrural joint A-P mobilization
				Closed chain talocrural joint A-P with belt
			Question:
				-Pre-test probability = 75%
					Intervention with low risk to patient
					Intervention easy to apply with little extra time or cost
				-Post-test probability = 95%
				-20% point increase in likelihood of success
				-Does the use of this CPR dramatically change the way you would address this patient, given the sample’s response to treatment?
				-Is there any reason why you would not apply the intervention without the CPR given you already have a 75% chance of success?
		Effect of Foot Orthoses for Plantar Fasciitis: Landorf KB (2006)
			Design:
				Aim to evaluate the short and long term effectiveness of foot orthoses in treatment of plantar fasciitis
			Methods:
				Participant blinded, RCT
				Duration of follow up was 12 months
				135 participants randomly assigned to one of three groups
					Sham orthosis ( soft, thin foam)
					Prefabricated orthosis (firm foam)
					Customized orthosis (semirigid plastic)
			Outcome Measures:
				Primary – 
					Pain and function at 3 and 12 months (Foot Health Status Questionnaire)
				Results: BOLD – not statistically significant, but clinically meaningful?
				ANCOVA – Adjusted Estimates of Effects ("ANCOVA – Adjusted Estimates of Effects.png")
			Conclusions:
				Pain
					No statistical differences however trend towards difference
					Use clinical decision making
					No screening for need for orthotics
				Function
					Significant at 3 months for prefab and customized
					Significance lost  at 12 months

Review of the Anatomy of the Foot and Ankle
	("Osteology of the Ankle & Foot.png")
		Fibula
			-The fibula provides boney stability to the lateral ankle, but otherwise there is no major function in weight bearing. The fibula serves mainly as an area for muscle attachment.  The lateral malleolus forms the outer walls of the mortise​.
			-Clincial notes:
				High velocity, high force trauma can result in fracture​.
				Lateral malleolar fracture with high force inversion > eversion ankle sprain​.
				Avulsion fracture at tip of lateral malleolus with inversion sprain​.
		Tibia
			-The tibia transmits the body’s weight to the foot.  The tibial shaft is thinnest at the junction of its middle and distal thirds.
			-There is an interosseous membrane between the tibia and fibula. 
			-Clinical notes:
				High velocity, high force trauma can result in fracture​.
				Medial Malleolar fracture with high force inversion or eversion ankle sprain​.
				Repetitive microtrauma can result in stress fractures​.
		Talus
			-The talus is one of the seven tarsal bones of the foot, and it forms the lower portion of the ankle joint proper. It also articulates with the calcaneal bone to form the subtalar joint. 
			-Clinical Notes:
				Osteochondral lesion to the dome of the talus with high velocity forces​
				Stress fracture from repetitive microtrauma​
				Subtalar instability with recurrent ankle sprains
		Navicular
			The navicular bone is one of the seven tarsal bones of the foot. 
			Clinical Notes:
				Tarsal instability at articulation with cuneiforms
		Cuneiform bones
			There are three cuneiform bones:
				First (medial) cuneiform.
				Second (intermediate cuneiform), also known as the middle cuneiform.
				Third (lateral) cuneiform.
				The cuneiforms contribute to three of the seven tarsals bones of the foot. 
			Clinical Notes:
				Tarsal instability ​
				Lis Franc fracture
		Calcaneus
			-The calcaneus (heel bone) is located in the hindfoot, and comprises one of the seven tarsal bones of the foot. It is also the largest bone of the foot.
			Clinical Notes:
				Fracture with high velocity/impact force​.
				Fracture/avulsion of growth plate.​
		Cuboid
			The cuboid is one of the seven tarsal bones of the foot.  
			Clinical Note:
				Can become unstable and subluxed with forced, high velocity inversion​
		Tarsals (Tarsus)
			The seven tarsal bones of the foot include: 
				Calcaneus
				Talus
				Navicular
				Cuneiforms
				Cuboid
			They collectively form the tarsus.
		Metatarsals
			-The metatarsals refer to the five long bones found in each foot, which connect the hind/mid-foot to the phalanges. The metatarsals are numbered with Roman numreals I to V (medial to lateral).
			-Clinical Notes:
				Styloid process of 5th metatarsal:​
				Prone to avulsion fracture from pull of peroneus brevis with inversion sprain​
				Jones fracture occurs just distal to styloid process and is prone to non-union​
		Phalanges
			-The phalanges are the bones of the toes. There are 14 phalanges in each foot.
			-The second to fifth toes contain a proximal, middle and distal phalanx. The big/great toe (hallux) only contains a proximal and distal phalanx.
			-Clinical Notes:
				Proximal Phalanges:​
					-The MTP joints are prone to osteoarthritis and loss of normal flexion and extension​.
					-1st MTP joint prone to “bunions” and Hallux Abducto Valgus​.
				Middle Phalanges:​
					-Hammer toes can result from tendon shortening resulting in forced, rigid flexion of the middle phalanx​.
	Arthrology of Ankle
		Talocrural Joint ("Ankle Joint Proper")
			-The talocrural joint or ankle mortise is the articulation of the concave distal tibia and fibula with the convex talar dome.
		Subtalar Joint
			The subtalar joint is the articulation between the talus and calcaneus.
		Tibiofibular Joint
			-The distal tibiofibular joint is the articulation between the tibia and fibula.  
			-Clinical note: The distal tibiofibular joint can become injured with forced dorsiflexion and eversion that results in the “high ankle sprain”​.
		---------------------------------------------------
		Regions of the Foot
			The foot can be sub-divided into two regions:
				Rear/hindfoot - includes the talus & calcaneus.
				Forefoot - includes all other bones of the foot.
		Joints of the Foot
			-There are several joints of the foot, but we are going to focus on two joints.
			-Specifically, the midtarsal joint has two articulations:
				1. Talo-navicular (medially).
				2. Calcaneal-cuboid (laterally).  
			-We call these two joints the midfoot.
			-The talonavicular and calcanealcuboid joint mark the line of separation between the rearfoot (talus and calcaneus) and the forefoot (everything distal).
	Arches of the Foot
		Medial & Lateral Longitudinal
			Medial Longitudinal Arch
				The medial longitudinal arch is very important as it must deal with entire body weight.
					The arch is built to distribute loads and is formed by the following bony structures.
					Distal to proximal the arch is formed by the: 1st metatarsal, 1st cuneiform, navicular, calcaneus.
				The medial longitudinal arch is supported by the bony architecture, many ligaments, and tendons.
			Lateral Longitudinal Arch
				-Corresponding to the medial longitudinal arch, there is the lateral longitudinal arch. Again, going from distal to proximal the arch is made up of the 5th metatarsal – cuboid – calcaneus.
		Transverse Arch
			The foot is also arched in the transverse direction.
				-Lateral to medial the transverse arch is made up of the cuboid – Lateral cuneiform – middle cuneiform – medial cuneiform
				-The arch helps to spread the weight in all directions.
			The bottom right illustration is a cross-section at the level of the MT joint, and the upper left illustration is at the tarsal joint.
	Common Postural Variations of the Medial Longitudinal Arch ("Disorders of the Foot Arches.png")
		-Common postural variations of the medial longitudinal arch are primarily:
		-Pes planus is an excessively flat foot that is associated with pronation. This is associated with a number of pathologies including overuse pathologies and issues related to instability of the subtalar and midtarsal joints.
		Pes cavus is an excessively high arch foot and is associated with excessive rigidity and supination of the foot. This is also associated with a number of pathologies, mostly related to overuse pathologies related to rigidity of the subtalar and midtarsal joints.
	Common Disorders related to the Transerse Arch
		Common conditions related to the transerve arch are primarily:
			-Related pathologies include Morton’s neuroma, pinch calluses, and metatarsal stress fractures.
				("01 AOA Orthopedic Specialists - What is Morton's Neuroma")

General Mobility Testing [MSK1_Imm2B_Ankle]
	Calcaneal Inversion/eversion ('Calcaneal Inversion eversion.png')
		The available passive ROM of the rearfoot is assessed with the patient in the long-sitting position
		Both Calcaneal bones are grasped (bilaterally) and then passively inverted and everted as much as possible
		A subjective determination is made whether the motion is normal, increased or decreased
	Fore/midfoot Abduction/adduction ('Fore midfoot Abduction adduction.png')
		-The available passive ROM of the midfoot along its oblique axis is assessed with the patient in the long-sitting position
		-The Calcaneal bone is first stabilized with one hand
		-The other hand grasped the 4th and 5th metatarsal heads from the medial side and then passively abducts and adducts the forefoot as much as possible
		-A subjective determination is made whether the motion is normal, increased, or decreased
	Fore/midfoot Inversion/eversion ('Fore midfoot Inversion eversion.png')
		-The available passive ROM of the midfoot along its longitudinal axis is assessed with the patient in the long-sitting position
		-The Calcaneal bone is first stabilized with one hand
		-The other hand grasped all metatarsal heads from the medial side and then passively inverts and everts the forefoot as much as possible
		-A subjective determination is made whether the motion is normal, increased or decreased

Manual Muscle Testing for the Knee [From KIN]
	-Apart from the plantar flexors, most of the smaller muscles surrounding the foot and ankle provide more control and coordination than power and strength
		 These come from the larger, more proximal muscles
	-Nonetheless, it is often important to test various foot & ankle muscles in isolation for the purpose of:
		Assessing for injury to those muscles
		Identifying if the muscles are capable of providing the needed control and coordination
		Determining if the nerve to that muscle is impaired
	-------------------------------
	Functional Screening
		Plantar Flexors
			-The plantar flexors (gastrocnemius and soleus) are relatively strong muscles. Therefore, if there is not obvious weakness during function or with routine MMT, to find more moderate impairments, it may be necessary to perform more functional testing.
			---------------------------------------------
			Having the patient toe walk away from the PT
					Assess the ability to maintain full plantarflexion when comparing side to side.
			To more specifically quantify
				For the Gastrocnemius
					Compare the patient’s ability to perform single leg heel raises 10 times bilaterally.
					Single/bilaterally: means one leg and later the other one.
				For the Soleus
					Compare the patient’s ability to perform single leg heel raises 10 times bilaterally with the knee slightly FLEXED.  
		Dorsiflexors
			Heel walk back in their direction
			To more specifically quantify

						---------------------------------------------------------------------- Ruperta
						An explanation of two key concepts in medical testing: sensitivity (Sn) and specificity (Sp), often used in diagnostic testing.
						1. Specificity (Sp):
						   - Sp refers to the ability of a test to correctly identify those without a particular condition (true negatives). 
						   - In simpler terms, it tells us how well a test can rule "in" a condition or diagnosis when the result is positive (P+).
						   - When a test has high specificity, it means that if the test says you have the condition (positive result), you are very likely to have it. It produces few false positives.
						   - In the provided explanation, "Sp – (P+) – In" means that a specific test, when the result is positive, is effective for confirming or ruling "in" a condition or diagnosis. This is because it produces few false negatives, thus making the positive result accurate.
						2. Sensitivity (Sn):
						   - Sn refers to the ability of a test to correctly identify those with a particular condition (true positives).
						   - It tells us how well a test can rule "out" a condition or diagnosis when the result is negative (N-).
						   - When a test has high sensitivity, it means that if the test says you don't have the condition (negative result), you are very likely not to have it. It produces few false negatives.
						   - In the provided explanation, "Sn – (N-) – Out" means that a sensitive test, when the result is negative, is effective for excluding or ruling "out" a condition or diagnosis. This is because it produces few false positives, thus making the negative result accurate.
						In summary, specificity is about the accuracy of positive results (ruling "in" a condition), while sensitivity is about the accuracy of negative results (ruling "out" a condition). Both are important characteristics of diagnostic tests, and they complement each other in providing a comprehensive assessment of a test's performance.

				--------------------------------------------------------------- Ruperta
				Tibia vertical: This refers to the alignment of the tibia bone in a vertical orientation when viewed from the front or back. Proper alignment of the tibia helps distribute weight evenly and ensures efficient force transmission during weight-bearing activities.

				Calcaneus vertical with 5° varus: The calcaneus, or heel bone, should be vertical when viewed from the side, but with a slight inclination of approximately 5 degrees in varus. Varus refers to the inward angulation or tilt of a body part, in this case, the calcaneus. This alignment facilitates proper weight distribution and stability during weight-bearing activities.
					In anatomical terms, "varus" typically refers to an angulation or tilt of a body part towards the midline of the body or away from the sagittal plane. So, if we're referring to the foot and ankle, a varus alignment would indeed mean that the angle is tilted outward from the body.

					Therefore, when discussing the alignment of the calcaneus with 5° varus, it implies that the calcaneus bone is tilted slightly outward from the vertical axis of the body when viewed from the rear or medial aspect. This slight outward tilt contributes to the normal biomechanics of the foot and ankle, providing stability and efficient weight distribution during weight-bearing activities.

				Forefoot perpendicular to the bisection of the calcaneus: The forefoot, which includes the metatarsals and toes, should be positioned perpendicular to an imaginary line that bisects the calcaneus bone. This alignment ensures proper weight distribution across the forefoot and facilitates efficient propulsion during gait.
					when we say the forefoot should be perpendicular to this bisection, it means that the front part of the foot (the forefoot), including the metatarsal bones and toes, should be oriented at a 90-degree angle relative to the imaginary line that bisects the calcaneus.

				10° true ankle joint dorsiflexion: Dorsiflexion refers to the movement of the foot and ankle complex where the toes move towards the shin. In this criterion, there should be a range of motion of approximately 10 degrees of dorsiflexion at the ankle joint. Sufficient dorsiflexion is essential for activities such as walking, running, and squatting, as it allows for adequate clearance of the foot during the swing phase of gait and facilitates proper weight transfer during activities such as squatting.

				1st ray in the plane of 2nd-4th rays: The first ray, which includes the first metatarsal and medial cuneiform bones, should be aligned in the same plane as the second to fourth metatarsals. Proper alignment of the first ray helps maintain the transverse arch of the foot and supports efficient weight distribution during weight-bearing activities.
					The "1st ray" refers to the bones that comprise the big toe (hallux), including the first metatarsal bone and its associated phalanges. The "2nd-4th rays" refer to the bones of the lesser toes, including the second, third, and fourth metatarsal bones and their associated phalanges.

					In an ideally aligned foot, the first metatarsal bone should lie in the same horizontal plane as the second to fourth metatarsal bones. This means that when viewed from a certain angle, such as from above or below, the first metatarsal bone should appear to be aligned in a straight line with the second to fourth metatarsals.

					This alignment is important for maintaining the transverse arch of the foot, which helps distribute weight evenly across the forefoot during weight-bearing activities such as standing, walking, and running. It also supports proper biomechanics of the foot and helps prevent issues such as excessive pressure and strain on specific areas of the foot, which can lead to discomfort or injury over time.

				70°-75° 1st MTP extension: The first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, which is the joint at the base of the big toe, should have a range of motion of approximately 70 to 75 degrees of extension. Adequate extension at this joint is important for activities such as push-off during walking and running, as well as for maintaining balance and stability during weight-bearing tasks.

					Stretching

8.9 Orthotics
	Biomechanical and Accommodative Orthoses
		Functions of Orthotic Devices
			The main goal should be to relieve stress on specific structures
			Motion will not be completely restricted, but you can make small changes with each step:
				Improve weight bearing distribution
				Control faulty biomechanics
				Improve shock absorption
				Relieve stress on healing structures
			----------------------------------------------------------------
			Two Basic Purposes of Foot Orthoses
				1. Control of Motion - functional orthosis
				2. Relieve Pressure - accommodate orthosis
				------------------------------------------------------------
				What is the function of an orthotic device?
					✓to control function and to accommodate
					to change structure and to accommodate
			Podiatric Theory of Foot Function ('Podiatric Theory of Foot Function.png')
				-The Podiatric Theory of Foot Function is centered around placing the foot in the ideal position of sub-talar neutral. The thought is that the foot functions optimally around subtalar neutral. Subtalar neutral is the position of no pronation or supination. This theory is based solely off of that position
					Subtalar neutral
						is defined as described below:
							Subtalar joint neither pronated nor supinated
							Head of talus equally palpable on both sides of talonavicular joint
							Midline of calcaneus not necessarily aligned with midline of leg
							2/3 supination, 1/3 pronation
			"Ideal Foot Alignment" ('Ideal Foot Alignment.png')
				-Another way consideration of the Podiatric Model is that the foot be placed in "ideal alignment, which should be that the midline of the calcaneus aligns with the midline of the lower leg (that's why you can measure down the leg). Parallel at lower leg and calcaneus and perpendicular in forefoot. But we are usually not in this position.
					When subtalar joint is in Neutral:
						Midline of calcaneus aligns with midline of lower leg
						Plane of forefoot perpendicular to midline of calcaneus

			Treatment-Direction Testing to Predict the Effectiveness of Foot/Ankle Orthotics
				-There is relatively poor correlation of the presence of foot posture abnormalities with the onset of foot and ankle pain
					-In other words, just because an individual has a notable abnormality does not mean that they will also have foot pain
					-Similarly, foot posture abnormalities are not always the source of a patient's symptoms as these may be due to other impairments such as decreased proximal strength, poor mechanics, etc.
					-Therefore, orthotics may or may not be effective in relieving a patient's pain
					-Because implementation of orthotics can be expensive and take considerable time, it is helpful to use treatment direction testing to predict which patients are most likely to benefit from orthotics
			The Reverse-6 Taping Technique ('The Reverse-6 Taping Technique.png')
				-This taping technique provides motion control in a similar manner to orthotics but is much quicker and less expensive. However, taping is not a long-term solution
				-Therefore, the PT may apply this taping method, have the patient engage in functional activities that typically produce symptoms, and assess if there is a significant reduction in pain Research suggests that if the patient experiences a 50% or greater reduction in symptoms then orthotics are likely to be of benefit
				-This technique has also been shown to have sufficient intra- and inter-rater reliability (see article)
					------------------------------------------------------------
					The Reverse-6 Taping Technique is effective for:
						Providing a long-term solution for a patient's foot pain
						Increasing mobility of a hypo-mobile foot
						✓Screening to determine if the use of orthotics are worth the time and effort
			Summary
				In summary, the focus of the Resting Position Theory with respect to implementation of orthotics:
					-is on controlling motion with reference to the natural structure of the patient's foot
					-as opposed to trying to place all patients in the "ideal" position of sub-talar neutral
					-It should be noted that evidence suggests that orthotic treatment approaches utilizing the Resting Position approach are more effective than those based on the Podiatric method

					What is the difference between treatment and intervention)
						The terms "treatment" and "intervention" are often used interchangeably, but they can have slightly different nuances:

						a) Treatment typically refers to the overall plan or approach designed to address a specific condition or problem. It encompasses the various therapeutic modalities, exercises, manual techniques, and other interventions that are implemented to achieve the desired outcomes. Treatment often implies a comprehensive and structured approach tailored to the individual needs of the patient.

						b) Intervention, on the other hand, refers to the specific actions or techniques applied within the treatment plan to address particular impairments or goals. It focuses on the implementation of therapeutic strategies aimed at improving function, reducing pain, restoring mobility, or achieving other rehabilitation objectives. Interventions can include exercises, manual therapy techniques, patient education, modalities such as heat or electrical stimulation, and various other therapeutic approaches.

						In summary, while treatment refers to the broader plan or strategy aimed at addressing a patient's condition, intervention refers to the specific actions or techniques implemented as part of that treatment plan.

				S&S_2.Signs and Symptoms
					-Pain on palpation: at insertion at the anterior medial calcaneal tubercle of plantar fascia
					-Typically complain of severe pain on weight bearing after period of rest or sleep, resolves temporarily as patient walks. (probably stiffness) Pain then re-appears after increased activity
					-Symptoms may be reproduced with passive extension of the MTPs or resisted flexion of the toes
					-Patient may have limited passive range of ankle DF and MTP DF
					*May have a variety of alignment issues that contribute to symptoms
						-If rigid foot = accommodate and shock absorb
						-If flexible foot = support arch and relieve stress
					-Pes cavus deformity or RF/FF varus deformity may be present with excessive pronators
					-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
					Windlass Effect
						-Mechanism that lifts the medial longitudinal arch during toe off
						-As toe extends, the plantar fascia lengthens and increases tension on the medial longitudinal arch
						-Increased tension pulls the arch together and raises the arch to stabilize the foot during toe-off
					Windlass Testing
						Purpose: to test for the presence of fascial impairments of the foot
						Test Position: Sitting and standing
						Performance:
							-Sitting – stabilize ankle in neutral with 1 hand just proximal to metatarsal head. Then, extend the first phalange while allowing IP joint to flex  
								-Positive test – passive extension is continued to end range or until the patient’s pain is reproduced
							-Standing – patient stands on a stool with metatarsal heads just off the edge of stool. Patient is instructed to place equal weight on both feet. Examiner extends the first phalange while allowing the IP joint to flex  
								-Positive test – passive extension is continued to end range or until the patient’s pain is reproduced
							*You need to have enough tension that the windlass effect is accomplished. But with plantar fascial pain, you often can not get to increase the tension as needed because of the pain
				Treatment
					-Inflammation: Pain relievers, icing, modifying activities that cause pain.
					-Stretching of ankle PFs, toe flexors, and plantar fascia (DiGiovanni, 2003).
					-Strengthening of toe flexors and intrinsics.
					-Deep friction massage to plantar fascia.
					-Orthotics to correct biomechanical dysfunctions.
					-Heel cups or donut pads are often prescribed but don’t usually have much success. They don’t control the biomechanical dysfunctions. They may, however, give temporary relief by helping with shock absorption and relieving pressure over the inflamed area.
					-Resting dorsiflexion night splint.
					-*Regarding results from DiGiovanni et al ‘03, ’06: 2 yr f/u 92% show total satisfaction regardless of type of stretching/treatment (somewhat self limiting)
					-*DiGiovanni BF et al. Tissue-specific plantar fascia-stretching exercise enhances outcomes in patients with chronic heel pain. Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery.  85(7): 1270-1277. r2003
				Plantar Fasciitis Intervention Guidelines
					(Martin RL, et al. JOSPT 2014)
					Orthopedic section of APTA’s evidenced based clinical practice guidelines
					-------------------------------------------------------------------------
					Manual Therapy (A, strong evidence)
						-Should use manual therapy consisting of joint and soft tissue mobilization STM to treat LE joint mobility and calf flexibility deficits and to decrease pain and improve function
						-STM techniques directed to musculature of the lower leg and calf
						-Pragmatically (Γ practical and hands-on approach) applied manual therapy to the hip, knee, ankle, and/or foot with follow-up hep Home Exercise Program (HEP) for self-mobilization
					Stretching (A, strong evidence)
						-Plantar fascia and calf muscle stretching 2-3 times per day to help provide short-term (1 week to 4 month) relief
						-Heel pads may be used to increase benefit of stretching
					Orthotic devices (A, strong evidence)
						-Prefabricated or custom-made can help provide short-term (2 weeks) to long-term (1 year) improvements. Need to support the MLA and cushion the heel. No difference noted between custom-made and prefabricated orthoses. Especially useful in those who respond to anti-pronation.
					Night splints (A, strong evidence)
						-To support the use if patient’s symptoms present > 6 months. Should be worn 1-3 months (especially for those who have pain with first step in the morning)
					Taping (A, strong evidence)
						-Clinicians should use anti-pronation taping for immediate (up to 3 weeks) pain reduction and improved function
						-Can also use elastic therapeutic tape applied to the gastroc and plantar fascia for short-term (1 week) pain reduction
					Modalities
						Electrotherapy – (D, conflicting evidence)
							-Dexamethasone (0.4%) or acetic acid (5%) via iontophoresis can provide short term relieve (2-4 weeks). – may or may not use for short term pain relief and improved function
							-Clinicians should use manual therapy, stretching, & foot orthoses instead of electrotherapy agents to promote intermediate to long-term (1-6 months) improvements
						Physical Agents – (C, weak evidence)
							-Low-Level Laser Therapy – (C, weak evidence) Clinicians may use low-laser therapy to reduce pain and activity limitations
							-Physical Agents – (C, weak evidence) Clinicians may use phonophoresis (AKA sonophoresis) with ketoprofen gel to reduce pain
							-Ultrasound – (C, weak evidence) The use of US can NOT be recommended for individuals with heel pain/plantar fasciitis
						Therapeutic Exercise and Neuromuscular Re-education 
							-May prescribe strengthening exercises and movement training for muscles that control pronation and attenuate forces during WB activities
						Dry Needling – Not recommended at this point

		8PathAnkl.AchTRup. Achilles Tendon (AT) Rupture​ Overview (Q&A_)
			-Generally associated with a degenerative process
			-Tendon weakens and a tear usually occurs in the area of least blood supply: 2-6 cm proximal to calcaneus (watershed region)
			-Incidence of injury increases with age, probably related to compromised blood supply
			-Also associated with systemic diseases such as: hyperthyroidism, systemic lupus erythematosus, syphilis, or tuberculosis
			--------------------------------------------------
			Mechanisms
				Mechanism: Indirect trauma
					Combo of overstretch and forceful muscle contraction
						Forceful push-off on extended knee – tendon gives
						Abrupt, excessive DF, such as the foot being pushed into DF on landing
							Falling off of curb or step or stepping into a hole are common mechanisms
						External blow to calcaneus while foot is in DF (tackle)
						PF during landing, resulting in increased contraction force of gastroc-soleus
						Over-supinators (rigid foot) tend to have increase tension on tendon
					Mechanism: Direct trauma
						Lacerations
						Gunshot wounds
			S&S_8.Signs & Symptoms
				-Typically middle aged (30-50 years)
				-C/O sudden snap, audible popping, may say they feel like they have been clubbed in the back of the leg
				-Pain is initially severe and may become more of a dull ache
				-Positive Thompson test
				-Less PF observed in prone lying on involved leg
				-Depression in area of tendon on inspection
				-Unable to raise up on toes of affected limb
				-Excessive DF during stance of gait, walk with flexed knee, difficulty negotiating stairs
				-Edema, ecchymosis
			Treatment Approaches for Achilles Tendon Rupture
				General Treatment Approaches
					-Older, inactive patients treated conservatively using casting and bracing
					-Younger, active patients typically treated surgically. There is better prognosis with surgical treatment for active individuals
			Operative Repair of Achilles Tendon
				Operative repair of the Achilles tendon involves the following:
					-Bringing the two ends of the ruptured achilles back together via suturing
					-Protecting the sutured area and placing a heel wedge underneath to keep excessive tension off of the tendon during healing
					----------------------------------------------------------
					Post-operative Treatment
						-Walking cast in PF, 0-3 weeks
						-Neutral DF walking cast 4-6 weeks (NWB or PWB until this point)
						-2 cm heel lift on shoe for 2 months post cast removal
						-Focus on gradual return of DF ROM (2°-5° at a time) and PF strength (can use joint mobilizations)
						-Proprioceptive and functional retraining activities
						-Return to activity in 6 months
			Conservative Treatment for Achilles Tendon Rupture
				Immobilized in PF 6-8 weeks
				Change cast every 2 weeks, adding more DF (lessening amount of heel wedge)
				Progress to walking cast when 2 weeks in neutral DF
				Flexibility for 1st 4 weeks after cast removal (not forceful)
				Strength training initiated at 4 weeks post cast removal
				Return to activity in 4-6 months
				*Important: Progress slowly, being mindful of the healing capacity of the tissue
			Case Study: Willits Protocol for Conservative Treatment of Achilles Tendon Rupture
				-Published studies have shown a benefit of early functional stimulus to healing tendons. The following information summarizes a study ('Operative versus Nonoperative.pdf') regarding operative and nonoperative accelerated functional rehabilitation outcomes of Achilles tendon ruptures
					Purpose
						-The purpose of study was to compare the outcomes of patients with an acute tendon rupture treated with operative repair and accelerated functional rehabilitation with the outcomes of similar patients treated with accelerated with functional rehabilitation alone
							Operative vs. Nonoperative
								All patients underwent an accelerated rehabilitation protocol 
								Featured early weight-bearing and early ROM
					Results
						Total of 144 participants (72 each group), random sampling
						Mean age 40.4 years
						Re-ruptured:
							Operative = 2
							Nonoperative = 3
						Strength:
							No clinically important differences between groups regarding strength, ROM, calf circumference
						Complications:
							Operative = 13
							Nonoperative = 6
					Conclusion
						-The study supports accelerated functional rehab and nonoperative treatment for acute Achilles tendon ruptures
					--------------------------------------------
					Which is true about the mechanism of an Achilles tendon rupture? 
						✓Can occur with an abrupt, excessive DF of the foot; the foot is pushed into DF on landing
						Can occur with PF during landing, resulting in increased contraction force of anterior tibialis

				S&S_11.↓¿?
				Pathology
					Degenerative changes of 1st MTP joint leading to:
						1) Decreased ROM (limitus) and eventually
						2) Ankylosis of the joint (rigidus)
					Usually a result of either structural or functional deformity of 1st ray
						-Structural: Rigid DF 1st ray or short metatarsal
						-Functional: Elevation of 1st metatarsal (hallux extension) during weight bearing due to excessive pronation
						-Both types disrupt arthrokinematics of 1st MTP joint, causing dorsal jamming on DF of the hallux. Over time, cartilage erodes and joint space narrows
					Differentiated from HAV by limited DF of straight hallux
					Common injury to great toe in athletics
				Treatment
					-In functional cases:
						-Treat the over pronation, restore joint motion, and address shortened Achilles’ tendon if present, as this may cause excessive use of MTP DF during end of stance phase
					-Structural issue may be treated with shoe modifications (rocker bottom shoe) to reduce the DF requirements at the MTP
					-Surgical correction is used for severe cases (excision of bone overgrowth or osteotomy of 1st MTP to correct alignment, silicon joint implants)
						-Restoration of 1st toe extension is difficult after 1st MTP surgery

				Hallux limitus/rigidus is different from hav Hallux Valgus (HAV) (See '22PathAnklLess.Bun.') by what characteristic?

						The main symptom is pain in the leg along the tibia (shinbone)

				(Γ)### Differences between Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome (Shin Splints) & Stress Fractures on the Shin:
					#### Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome (Shin Splints):
					- Definition: Inflammation of muscles, tendons, and bone tissue around the tibia.
					- Location of Pain: Along the inner edge of the shinbone.
					- Characteristics: Shooting, aching pain that improves with rest; pain along the entire shinbone when touched.
					- Causes: Overuse, sudden changes in physical activity, poor conditioning, and footwear.
					- Treatment: Rest, ice, NSAIDs, stretching, strengthening exercises, and gradual return to activity.

					#### Stress Fractures:
					- Definition: Very small crack or group of cracks in the bone itself.
					- Location of Pain: Specific spots along the shin bone.
					- Characteristics: Deep, throbbing pain; painful with regular activities like walking or sitting.
					- Causes: Overuse, impact activities, low vitamin D levels, and poor dietary intake.
					- Treatment: Rest from aggravating activity for 6-8 weeks, immobilization if needed, gradual return to training.

					#### Differentiating Factors:
					- Pain Location: Shin splints have diffuse pain along the shinbone; stress fractures have specific focal pain.
					- Pain Characteristics: Shin splints are described as a dull ache; stress fractures cause a deep throbbing pain.
					- Activity Impact: Shin splint pain worsens after running or weight-bearing; stress fracture pain is immediate on weight-bearing and worsens with impact activities.

					In summary, while both conditions involve lower leg pain, shin splints are an inflammation of tissues around the tibia due to overuse, whereas stress fractures are small cracks in the bone itself caused by repetitive stress.

						Tendinitis and tendinosis are both conditions that affect tendons, but they have distinct characteristics that can help differentiate between them:

						1. Tendinitis:
						   - Inflammation: Tendinitis typically involves inflammation of the tendon, often due to overuse or acute injury.
						   - Symptoms: Symptoms may include pain, swelling, and tenderness around the affected tendon. The pain is usually more acute and may worsen with activity.
						   - Duration: Tendinitis tends to develop relatively quickly, and symptoms may resolve with rest and appropriate treatment.

						2. Tendinosis:
						   - Degeneration: Tendinosis is characterized by degeneration or breakdown of the tendon tissue, rather than inflammation.
						   - Symptoms: Symptoms are often more chronic and may include pain, stiffness, and weakness around the affected tendon. The pain may be more persistent and not necessarily worsen with activity.
						   - Duration: Tendinosis develops over time, often as a result of repetitive stress or overuse. It may require longer-term management and rehabilitation compared to tendinitis.

						To differentiate between the two conditions, a thorough clinical evaluation, including a patient's history, physical examination, and possibly imaging studies like ultrasound or MRI, may be necessary. Additionally, response to treatment can also provide clues, as tendinitis often responds well to anti-inflammatory measures, while tendinosis may require more targeted interventions focused on tissue healing and rehabilitation.

						-----------------------------------------------------------------------(Γ)
						Monofilament and biothesiometer testing are methods used to assess sensory perception in diabetic patients, particularly regarding their foot sensation.

						Monofilament testing involves the use of a nylon monofilament, which is applied to specific areas of the foot with varying degrees of pressure. The patient is asked to indicate whether they can feel the monofilament touching their skin. This test helps determine if the patient has lost protective sensation, which can be indicative of neuropathy.

						Biothesiometer testing involves the use of a device called a biothesiometer, which delivers controlled vibrations to the skin's surface. The patient typically feels the vibrations and reports their perception of the intensity. This test helps assess vibratory perception threshold and can detect early signs of sensory neuropathy in diabetic patients.

	Treatment Direction Testing ('Treatment Direction Testing.png')
		-(Γ)In simple terms, when therapists are trying to figure out if a certain treatment, like using orthotics for foot problems, will work for a patient, they are essentially guessing the chances of success before they start. They might think the treatment will work with a certain level of certainty, like being 75% or 80% sure. Therapists have a threshold level of certainty in mind before they decide to start treatment, considering how sure they need to be that the treatment will be effective. This decision is influenced by factors like the costs of being wrong versus the benefits of being correct. For example, if a treatment is low-cost and has few side effects, therapists might only need to be 50% sure it will work before starting it. The patient's preferences and values also play a role in deciding when to start a treatment. Therapists use tests and assessments to help them make these decisions and aim to link treatments with examination findings based on evidence-based practices

	Philosophies for Crafting Orthotics (Q&A_3lokijj)
		Podiatric Philosophy/Model (Old) 
			Based on creating orthotics that maintain subtalar neutral position
			Plaster casting used to create very rigid orthotic
			Extreme approach to motion control and support
			Expensive and variable results
		Custom Off-the-Shelf Orthotics (New) 
			Rigid to Semi-Rigid Inserts
			Provide support, shock absorption, and/or motion control motion
			Do not attempt to maintain absolute subtalar neutral position
			Variations for differing purposes

	Inserts
		Prefabricated
			-These are "off-the-shelf",,,not custom made. Generally less expensive and a good place to start for a lot of people. This is a good way to see if they may work
			-Prefabricated inserts are:
				Generally used as replacement for existing inserts
				Usually slightly raised in arch area
				Can add components onto it:
					Metatarsal pads
					Arch supports
					Heel cups
				Usually for shock absorption/pressure relief/comfort
				Has little biomechanical control
		Custom Made (Q&A_ggyyt54rds)
			Accommodative
				Shock absorption
				Pressure relief
				Little attempt at motion control
			Functional/Biomechanical (motion control) (Q&A_-ggtd4321l)
				Functional/Biomechanical Orthosis
					Will not correct the structural deformity
					Goal is to correct the biomechanical function
					This is done by adding posts (wedges) to orthotic in strategically placed positions
					The post brings the floor closer to the foot to reduce the amount of compensation
					Also look for total contact throughout arch to help support
			---------------------------------------------------------
				(Γ)What is the difference between "motion control" and "Functional/Biomechanical (motion control)" in Custom Made Inserts?
				The difference between "motion control" and "Functional/Biomechanical (motion control)" in Custom Made Inserts lies in the level of control and correction they offer:

				- Motion Control: This term generally refers to orthotics designed to control abnormal motion without a specific focus on correction. They aim to manage foot pain caused by various conditions by controlling motion but may not provide extensive correction[2].

				- Functional/Biomechanical (motion control): In contrast, this category of custom-made inserts is specifically designed to control the biomechanics of the foot and provide correction. These inserts focus on addressing foot issues by controlling motion and providing correction where needed, offering a more comprehensive approach compared to standard motion control inserts[3].

				This distinction highlights that while both types aim to manage foot motion, the Functional/Biomechanical (motion control) inserts go further by addressing biomechanical issues and providing corrective measures for improved foot function.
