6.0. Introduction to Module 6
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

-In this module, you will review the anatomical structures and biomechanics of the cervical spine. You will also learn about screening and examination techniques pertaining to the upper quarter. The module will conclude with an explanation of treatment techniques and conditions associated with the neck region.

Learning Objectives
	After completing this module, you should be able to:
		Identify “at-risk” cervical spine patients.
		Identify patients with “red flag” conditions of the cervical spine.
		Identify conditions that require need for a referral.
		Complete screening procedures for the vascular structures supplying the CNS.
		Apply functional anatomy to examine the mid-cervical spine.
		Complete movement testing of the mid-cervical spine.
		Identify and classify neck pain based on mobility and centralization of symptoms.
		Perform mobility testing of the entire cervical spine.
		Explain examination and treatment considerations at the upper cervical spine.
		Identify the need for traction and apply the same to the mid-cervical spine.
		Explain operative procedures of the cervical spine and principles of post-operative rehab.
		Perform various mid-cervical and lower cervical treatment techniques.
		Explain and identify cervical stenosis signs and symptoms.

		The C1 vertebra is also known as the axis.
			The C1 vertebra is known as the atlas. C2 is the axis.

Biomechanical Review
	-Recall that the cervical spine is comprised of two anatomical "segments", each of which function to produce movement in the cervical spine (upper & lower c-spine).  The cervical portion of the spine allows for much more mobility when comparing it to the thoracic and lumbar regions of the spine.
	-Movements possible at the cervical spine include: ('https://youtu.be/NfFzdBC5Jz4')
		Flexion and extension of the head
		Sidebending (lateral bending)
		Rotation

	Review the following movements associated with the upper and lower cervical regions:
		Atlanto-Occipital Joints - Upper Cervical (Occiput on C1)
			-This region consists of two occipital joints that act as one joint to create nodding (transverse axis) and slight sidebending (ant-post axis) movements.
				-Flexion: The occiput rolls anteriorly and glides posteriorly to move into flexion (~10 degrees). The opposite arthokinematics occur to create extension (~25 degrees).
				-Sidebending (~5 degrees): The ipsilateral occipital condyle glides anteriorly, medially & inferiorly. Concurrently, the opposite motions occur on the contralateral occipital condyle with slight rotation.
		Atlanto-Axial (A/A) Joints - Upper Cervical (C1 on C2)
			-In this region, movement occurs at the one median and two lateral A/A joints simultaneously. The lateral joints support the head's weight via the atlas, while the medial joint guides rotation.
				-Rotation: The motion is primarily rotational (~45 degrees), where the atlas' inferior facet glides forward on the contralateral side, and the facet glides backward on the ipsilateral side.
				-Flexion/Extension: Minimal flexion/extension (~10 degrees) occurs at these joints. During flexion, the atlas' inferior facets roll anteriorly, glide posteriorly and the atlas' anterior arch glides inferiorly. The opposite motions occur during extension.
		Lower Cervical (C3-C7)
			The associated mechanics of these segments is as follows:
				-Movement occurs at uncovertebral, intervertebral, and facet joints.
				-The uncovertebral joints allows the upper vertebra to rotate in relation to the the lower vertebra.
				-Intervertebral joints create the flexion/extension motion and are guided by uncovertebral joints. The intervertebral joints also create sidebending that is coupled with rotation in plane with the facet joints.
					-Flexion: Both upper facets moving superiorly and anteriorly on the the lower facets. The opposite arthokinematics occur to create extension.
					-Combined sidebending w/ rotation: Sidebending and rotation occur on the same side together. When turning to right, the right upper facet glides inferiorly and posteriorly (closing down), while the left upper facet glides superiorly and anteriorly (opening up).

		-Download the following slides for additional review of the arthrology and biomechanics associated with the upper ('Mod 6_Review of Cervical Arthrology and Biomechanics_Upper.pptx')(!!) and lower ('Mod 6_Review of Cervical Arthrology and Biomechanics_Upper.pptx')(!!) cervical regions. These slides are background information to use as needed.

			Capsular and Non-Capsular Patterns
				Description
					-It was James Cyriax who along with other authors developed the idea of the scanning examination. That was the origin of the concepts of “contractile” and “inert” tissue, “end feel,” and “capsular patterns” and this contributed greatly to the development of a comprehensive and systematic physical examination of the moving parts of the body.
					-Broadly speaking, there are two patterns of range of motion used in the interpretation of joint motion:
						-A capsular pattern of restriction is a limitation of pain and movement in a joint specific ratio, which is usually present with arthritis, or following prolonged immobilization.
						-A noncapsular pattern of restriction is a limitation in a joint in any pattern other than a capsular one, and may indicate the presence of either a derangement, a restriction of one part of the joint capsule, or an extra-articular lesion, that obstructs joint motion.
				Capsular Pattern
					-How to examine Capsular Pattern? With passive movement, a full ROM must be carried out in all possible directions. A mid-range movement will not elicit potential findings. While evaluating the end feel, the examiner must look at the pattern of limitation or restriction.
						Γ This is why we push at end range, during AROM, if full AROM is not achieve. This is considered PROM or AAROM but does not substitute PROM examination. ¿?
					-According to Dutton, capsular patterns are based on clinical findings rather than research; perhaps that's why the capsular patterns may be different or inconsistent.
					-There will be a presence of a typical pattern in the joint, if the capsule of the joint is affected. This pattern could be a resultant of joint reaction with muscle spasm that leads to capsular constriction. There could be osteophyte formation too as a possible factor for restriction. Each joint has a characteristic pattern of limitation. Capsular pattern cannot judge the endfeel. Only the joints controlled by muscles exhibit capsular pattern. E.g. The distal tibiofibular joint and the Sacroiliac Joint does not have any capsular pattern. A study done by Hayes et al explained the usefulness of pattern of limitation, but they also pointed that the proportion of limitation should not be used.
					('Joint_Capsular_Patter.jpg')
				Non-Capsular Patterns
					-A clinician should be aware about the joint limitation that exists but isn't capsular in nature. For example in the shoulder joint in case of subacromial bursitis, abduction may be restricted but with minimal restriction in rotation component of joint. In such cases the capsular reaction may not be exhibited but other tissues such as ligaments could get adhered. There could be restriction in just one movement or direction with pain where other directions or movements remain pain free with full range of motion. Other possibilities for joint restriction in one or more direction can be because of loose bodies and/or extra articular adhesions which does not affect the capsule. Hence non-capsular pattern.

		RUPERTA -------------------------------------------------------------------
			Understanding Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS) Treatment

			Key Impairments Contributing to Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS)

			Thoracic Outlet Syndrome involves neurovascular compression in the thoracic outlet region. Effective treatment relies on identifying and addressing specific impairments:

			1. Postural Abnormalities:
			   - Forward Head Posture: This can lead to tension in the cervical and thoracic spine, compressing neurovascular structures.
			   - Rounded Shoulders: This can tighten pectoral muscles and affect shoulder biomechanics.
			   - Increased Kyphosis: Excessive curvature of the thoracic spine can compress neurovascular structures.

			2. Weakness/Lack of Recruitment of Postural Muscles:
			   - Serratus Anterior: Stabilizes the scapula; weakness can lead to improper shoulder mechanics.
			   - Rhomboids: Retract the scapula; weakness can contribute to poor posture.
			   - Lower Trapezius: Helps scapular depression and retraction; weakness affects shoulder stability.
			   - Deep Cervical Flexors: Maintain head and neck posture; weakness contributes to forward head posture.
			   - Core Musculature: Provides trunk stability; weakness affects overall posture.

			3. Lack of Flexibility in Surrounding Soft Tissue Structures:
			   - Pectoralis Minor/Major: Tightness can compress neurovascular structures.
			   - Scalenes: Tightness can compress the brachial plexus.
			   - Upper Trapezius: Tightness can affect scapular positioning and contribute to neurovascular compression.

			4. Decreased Joint Mobility:
			   - Glenohumeral Joint: Limited mobility can affect shoulder mechanics.
			   - Cervical Spine: Restricted motion can lead to compensatory movements and increased compression.
			   - Upper Thoracic Spine: Reduced mobility can affect posture and biomechanics.
			   - 1st Rib: Elevation or hypomobility can compress neurovascular structures.

			5. Adverse Neural Tension:
			   - Neural tension in upper extremity peripheral nerves can exacerbate symptoms of TOS.

			Preparation for Class Discussion

			Why These Impairments Cause TOS Symptoms:

			- Postural Abnormalities: Misalignment of the head, shoulders, and spine alters the space within the thoracic outlet, increasing the likelihood of neurovascular compression.
			- Weakness/Lack of Recruitment of Postural Muscles: Weak muscles fail to support proper posture and scapular positioning, leading to biomechanical imbalances and compression.
			- Lack of Flexibility: Tight muscles reduce space in the thoracic outlet, directly contributing to compression of neurovascular structures.
			- Decreased Joint Mobility: Joint restrictions cause compensatory postures and movements that narrow the thoracic outlet.
			- Adverse Neural Tension: Tight neural structures increase sensitivity and exacerbate compressive symptoms.

			Specific Exercises to Address Each Impairment:

			1. Postural Abnormalities:
			   - Chin Tucks: Corrects forward head posture.
			   - Scapular Retractions: Improves shoulder positioning.
			   - Thoracic Extensions: Reduces kyphosis.

			2. Weakness/Lack of Recruitment:
			   - Serratus Punches: Strengthens serratus anterior.
			   - Prone T/Y/W Exercises: Targets rhomboids and lower trapezius.
			   - Deep Cervical Flexor Exercises: Enhances cervical spine stability.
			   - Planks and Bridges: Strengthen core musculature.

			3. Flexibility:
			   - Pectoral Stretches: Increases pectoralis minor/major flexibility.
			   - Scalene Stretches: Reduces tension in scalenes.
			   - Upper Trapezius Stretch: Alleviates tightness.

			4. Joint Mobility:
			   - Shoulder Dislocations with a Band: Improves glenohumeral mobility.
			   - Cervical Spine ROM Exercises: Enhances cervical mobility.
			   - Thoracic Spine Mobilizations: Increases upper thoracic flexibility.
			   - 1st Rib Mobilizations: Restores proper rib positioning.

			5. Neural Tension:
			   - Nerve Glides: Reduce neural tension in the upper extremity.
