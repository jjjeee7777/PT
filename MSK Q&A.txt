3. Which foot deformity may predispose a patient to shortened Achilles tendon?
   a) Pes planus
   b) Pes cavus
   c) Hammer toe

   Answer: b) Pes cavus
   		How Pes Cavus predisposes a patient to shortened Achilles tendon?
   			Pes cavus, also known as high-arched foot, is a condition where the foot has an abnormally high plantar longitudinal arch¹. This condition can lead to a shortened Achilles tendon due to several factors:

			1. Muscle Imbalance: Pes cavus can be caused by muscle weakness and imbalance in neuromuscular diseases¹. This imbalance can lead to changes in the foot's structure, including the shortening of the Achilles tendon¹.

			2. Hindfoot Deformity: In some cases, pes cavus is associated with a hindfoot deformity, where the rear foot is in varus (inverted) position, the subtalar joint is supinated, and the calcaneocuboid joint is rigid³. This often co-exists with a shortened Achilles tendon, and the pull of the Achilles on a varus calcaneus worsens the cavus form³.
				('rear foot varus.(inverted) position') ('rear foot varus.(inverted) position 02')

			3. Contracture of the Plantar Fascia: The plantar fascia, a thick band of tissue that connects the heel bone to the toes, can become contracted in pes cavus¹. This can indirectly lead to the shortening of the Achilles tendon¹.

			It's important to note that these changes can cause various symptoms such as pain, instability, and difficulty walking. If you or someone else has these symptoms, it's recommended to seek medical advice for proper diagnosis and treatment.

7. How many sets of eccentric resistance exercises are recommended per day?
   a) 1 set
   b) 2 sets
   c) 3 sets

   Answer: c) 3 sets of 15; 2x/day; 12 weeks – Strong Evidence
   		How can I learn more about sets, repetitions, and times per day respect to rehabilitation in Physical Therapy; per muscle or condition?
			1. Sets: A set refers to a group of repetitions. For example, if you complete 12 repetitions of an exercise, take a short rest, and then complete another 12 repetitions, this equals 2 sets¹.

			2. Repetitions (Reps): A repetition is one complete movement of an exercise. For example, if you lift a weight up and down once, that's one repetition¹.

			3. Starting Point: If you're new to strengthening exercises or haven't done them in a while, starting with 1-2 sets of each exercise consisting of 8-12 reps is a good place to start¹. You can gradually increase to completing 3-4 sets once you can comfortably complete 2 sets of 12 reps¹.

			4. Non-Rehab Strength Training: The American College of Sports Medicine provides various formulas for general strengthening when there are no injuries present¹:
			    - 4-6 reps for heavy weights to increase muscle size (hypertrophy)
			    - 8-12 reps for general strengthening
			    - 10-15 reps for muscular endurance¹

			5. Rehab Strength Training: Rehab exercises are not aiming to bulk up muscles, but are instead meant to help retrain the muscles to work in a more functional way¹. Once the issue is being better managed or improved, we can then work on building muscles¹.

			6. Frequency: Rehab exercises can be completed more frequently than heavier strength training, as they're not as stressful on your body¹. Generally, rehab exercises can be completed 1-3 times per day¹.

			7. Adjustments: Sometimes reps, sets, and frequency will change from day to day depending on how your body is reacting, like increased soreness, increased pain, no pain or soreness, swelling, etc¹.

			8. Resistance: Generally speaking, with rehab exercises you can use a resistance or weight that allows you to complete 10-20 reps over 1-2 sets³. When you can easily complete 25-30 reps over 2-3 sets, you’re ready to increase the resistance³.

    	About expert opinion level evidence
    		When an intervention has "expert opinion level evidence" supporting it, it means that the recommendation or practice is based primarily on the collective judgment and experience of experts in the field rather than on empirical evidence from rigorous scientific studies. In the hierarchy of evidence-based medicine, expert opinion is considered one of the lowest levels of evidence due to its subjective nature and lack of empirical validation through research.

			Expert opinion is often relied upon when there is insufficient or conflicting empirical evidence to guide clinical decision-making. It may be derived from consensus statements, expert panels, or clinical practice guidelines developed by experienced practitioners who have extensive knowledge and expertise in a particular area of healthcare.

			While interventions supported by expert opinion may lack strong empirical evidence, they are still considered valuable in guiding clinical practice, especially in situations where there is uncertainty or limited available evidence. However, it's important for practitioners to recognize the limitations of expert opinion and to incorporate it judiciously alongside other forms of evidence when making clinical decisions.

(Q&A_3PathAnkl.Over3.PostTibTen. Posterior Tibialis Tendinopathy Overview)
How can rearfoot and forefoot varus deformities affect Posterior Tibialis?
	Rearfoot and forefoot varus deformities can significantly impact the function of the posterior tibialis muscle. When a person has these deformities, it means that their foot tends to roll inward excessively during the stance phase of gait, a condition known as overpronation. This excessive pronation places increased stress on the posterior tibialis tendon as it tries to control and stabilize the foot. 

	Specifically, in individuals with rearfoot varus deformity, the heel bone (calcaneus) tilts inward, causing the subtalar joint to excessively pronate. This puts additional strain on the posterior tibialis muscle, which is responsible for maintaining the arch of the foot and controlling pronation. As a result, the posterior tibialis tendon may become overworked and irritated, leading to tendinopathy or tendonitis.

	Similarly, forefoot varus deformity involves the front part of the foot tilting inward, causing further exacerbation of overpronation and increasing stress on the posterior tibialis tendon. These deformities can disrupt the normal biomechanics of the foot, leading to altered gait patterns and increased risk of injury to the posterior tibialis tendon. Therefore, addressing these deformities through appropriate interventions such as orthotics or corrective exercises is essential in managing posterior tibialis tendinopathy and restoring proper foot mechanics.

	Plantar Fascitis
		-Pain on palpation: at insertion at the anterior medial calcaneal tubercle of plantar fascia
		-Typically complain of severe pain on weight bearing after period of rest or sleep, resolves temporarily as patient walks. (probably stiffness) Pain then re-appears after increased activity
		-Symptoms may be reproduced with passive extension of the MTPs or resisted flexion of the toes
		-Patient may have limited passive range of ankle DF and MTP DF
		*May have a variety of alignment issues that contribute to symptoms
			-If rigid foot = accommodate and shock absorb
			-If flexible foot = support arch and relieve stress
		-Pes cavus deformity or RF/FF varus deformity may be present with excessive pronators
	Posterior Tibialis Tendinopathy
		-Pain located posterior to medial malleolus, may extend proximally up distal medial tibia or extend distally along medial longitudinal arch
		-Pain exacerbated by level of activity, may resolve with rest
		-Limited dorsiflexion and eversion range with pain
		-Pain and possibly weakness on resisted plantar flexion and inversion
		-May have rearfoot and forefoot varus (no serà valgus ¿?) deformities ('Bringing the floor up to the foot.png'), or pes planus, resulting in excessive pronation
		-Recall that the posterior tibialis helps to support the medial longitudinal arch of the foot
			-Dysfunction of this can be very chronic
			-Excessive pronation can be a culprit of this
				-Think of what this muscle does and how pronation would affect it
	Peroneal Tendonitis
		May have supinated feet
		Pain located in lateral ankle, directly over tendon
		-----------------------------------------------------------
		Findings from ROM Assessment:
			AROM/PROM eversion painful and possibly weak (PROM weak? ¿?)
			Tendons may sublux
			PROM painful inversion and dorsiflexion (and in inversion too? ¿?)
		Palpation:
			Tender over peroneal tendons
		Special Tests:
			If related to ankle sprain or instability – may have positive instability tests
	Inversion (Lateral) Ankle Sprains
		Tenderness to palpation of lateral collateral ligaments
		Swelling and possibly discoloration (ecchymosis) in lateral ankle and foot
		Painful limitation of PF/Inv
		Possible painful and weak resisted eversion = peroneus tendon injury
		Positive anterior drawer and medial talar tilt tests
		Antalgic gait, limping due to decreased weight bearing and limited ROM
		Make sure you clear for fracture (Ottawa ankle rules)
	Syndesmotic Sprains
		-Tenderness to palpation of distal tib-fib region
		-Swelling may be minimal at times, which may mislead the examiner into thinking there is no severe injury
		-Patients will avoid full DF during gait to minimize stress on distal tib-fib joint. May walk with steppage gait (Not allowing normal DF range)
			Γ('Foot Drop steppage gait.mp4')
			Γ('Steppage and Foot Slap Gait Foot Drop.mp4')
		-Passive full DF may reproduce symptoms (Γsince opens/forces/stretches the tibiofibular joint)
		-External rotation test will be positive (with foot in neutral DF, knee flexed to 90°, examiner externally rotates foot) (Kleiger’s Test)
		-Distal Tib-fib compression test will reproduce pain
		----------------------------------------------------
		-Palpation is very important
		-Normal DF ROM = causes joint to separate a little bit, so they may walk with a steppage gait because DF hurts
		-ER test = foot in neutral DF, ER foot = does it reproduce pain?
		-T-F compression test = can perform over the distal tib-fib area or can perform superiorly to cause bowstring action at the distal tib-fib region (probably better to perform it superiorly b/c at the level you are just compressing the joint, not stressing it)
	Chronic Ankle Instability (CAI)
		-10–20% continue to suffer from lateral ankle instability after an acute ligament injury
		-Recurrent sprains or giving way lasting longer than 6 months
		-Can occur with or without increased mechanical laxity (without = “functional”)
		-CAI – caused by disturbed proprioception. Treated conservatively with PT 
			What would you do to treat this?
				-PT aimed at coordination and strength of muscles around ankle to restore proprioception and thus stability
		-Caused by mechanical or functional deficits
			This may lead to a more motor control issue that just a strenght output issue
	Achilles Tendon (AT)
		-Typically middle aged (30-50 years)
		-C/O sudden snap, audible popping, may say they feel like they have been clubbed in the back of the leg
		-Pain is initially severe and may become more of a dull ache
		-Positive Thompson test
		-Less PF observed in prone lying on involved leg
		-Depression in area of tendon on inspection
		-Unable to raise up on toes of affected limb
		-Excessive DF during stance of gait, walk with flexed knee, difficulty negotiating stairs
		-Edema, ecchymosis
	Tarsal Tunnel Syndrome
		-Patient will complain of shooting, burning, pain along plantar aspect of foot and heel (distal distribution of tibial nerve)
		-May complain of parasthesias and/or impaired sensation on plantar aspect of foot
		-Symptoms increase with activity and decrease with rest
		-May have positive Tinel’s sign over tarsal tunnel
		-Stress with maximal DF/EV and MTP/IP extension held for 10 seconds
	Morton’s Neuroma
		Metatarsalgia of III and IV digit most common complaint
		Pain w/ weight bearing activities
		Paresthesias or impaired sensation in affected area
	Hallux Rigidus/Hallux Limitus
		Degenerative changes of 1st MTP joint leading to:
			1) Decreased ROM (limitus) and eventually
			2) Ankylosis of the joint (rigidus)
		Usually a result of either structural or functional deformity of 1st ray
			-Structural: Rigid DF 1st ray or short metatarsal
			-Functional: Elevation of 1st metatarsal (hallux extension) during weight bearing due to excessive pronation
			-Both types disrupt arthrokinematics of 1st MTP joint, causing dorsal jamming on DF of the hallux. Over time, cartilage erodes and joint space narrows
		Differentiated from HAV by limited DF of straight hallux
		Common injury to great toe in athletics
	Turf toe
		Pain on MTP tibial aspect & w/ MTP extension & valgus stress

Types of Off-the-Shelf orthotics
	Off-the-shelf orthotics come in two main types: Prefabricated and Custom Functional[1][2]. Prefabricated orthotics, also known as arch supports, are non-custom devices designed to provide gentle support to the arch of the foot. They are less supportive than custom orthotics but can work well for many people at a lower cost. These orthotics can be found in retail stores, doctors' offices, and online, with prices typically ranging from $40.00 to $70.00[1][2].

	Custom Functional orthotics, on the other hand, are professional custom-made orthotics that are tailored to an individual's specific needs. They involve a thorough health history assessment, diagnosis, determination of materials, and an impression mold of the feet to create orthotics specifically for the individual. Custom Functional orthotics are recommended for those with specific needs or severe foot problems like severely flat feet[3].

Basics of posting
	What is it?
	Posting in orthotics refers to the addition of wedges or pads strategically placed within the orthotic device to modify foot alignment and function. These posts can be made from various materials such as foam, cork, or rigid plastics and are customized to address specific biomechanical issues in the foot. By adjusting the angles and heights of these posts, orthotists can influence how the foot interacts with the ground during walking or standing.

	Why do you do it?
	Posting is done in orthotics to achieve several objectives:
		1. Correcting Biomechanical Alignment: By strategically placing posts, orthotists can help align the foot and ankle properly, addressing issues like overpronation or supination.
		2. Reducing Compensation: Posts can bring the ground closer to the foot, reducing the need for compensatory movements that may lead to pain or injury.
		3. Improving Weight Distribution: Properly positioned posts can help distribute weight more evenly across the foot, reducing pressure on specific areas.
		4. Enhancing Stability: Posts can provide additional support and stability to areas of the foot that may be weak or prone to excessive motion.
		5. Aiding in Foot Function: By controlling motion and providing support, posting can help improve overall foot function and biomechanics.

	In summary, posting in orthotics is a technique used to customize and optimize foot alignment, function, and comfort by strategically adding wedges or pads within the orthotic device. It plays a crucial role in addressing biomechanical issues and improving overall foot health and performance.
