		Passive Movement of the Glenohumeral Joint
			A study by Harryman et al. in 1990 threw the concave-convex rule into question, especially as it relates to passive movement in the glenohumeral joint. ​
				They found that capsular tightness alters the kinematics.  
					Specificallly that:
						Posterior capsular tightness causes anterior translation with flexion, opposite of what we would expect
						They also found posterior translation with extension, the opposite of what you expect
			Ultimately, this highlights the function of the rotator cuff in creating “normal arthrokinematics”. You can observe the findings of the study in the graph below. 

			('Passive Movement of the Glenohumeral Joint.png')

			Influences on Glenohumeral Motion
				To influence the GHJ motion, other factors are needed such as:
					Passive restraints including the capsule, ligaments, and intra-articular pressure
					Active restraints – rotator cuff

				The shoulder girdle is mostly designed for mobility at the expense of stability, which causes the articulation to inherently unstable and frequently dislocated.

	Associated Joints
		Acromioclavicular Joint
			The acromioclavicular (AC) joint is a plane synovial joint that assists in raising the arm above the head.
			It functions as a pivot joint, though technically it is a gliding synovial joint.
				The AC joint is mobile when the shoulder is overhead and across the chest.
				The joint is filled with a thick pad of cartilage, known as the meniscus, which allows the joint to move.
			The AC joint permits subtle movements between the scapula and lateral end of the clavicle.
				-Primary motion that is allowed is upward and downward rotation or (spin).
				-Upward rotation of the scapula at the AC joint occurs as the scapula “swings upwardly and outwardly” relative to the lateral end of the clavicle and this occurs as natural component of abduction or flexion.
				-Downward rotation returns the scapula back to its anatomic position.

		Sternoclavicular Joint
			The sternoclavicular (SC) joint is a sellar joint that links the appendicular skeleton with the axial skeleton.
				-Due to the articulations present and the joint orientations, the arthrokinematics are different in the superior/inferior direction vs. the anterior/posterior direction.
					-In the superior/inferior direction, there is a convex proximal clavicle moving on a concave manubrium, so the roll and glides will be opposite.
					-While in the anterior and posterior direction, the proximal clavicle is concave and the manubrium is convex, therefore the roll and glides are in the same direction.
				-The SC joint initiates and causes motion at the acromioclavicular (AC) joint, meaning the SC joint “unlocks” motion at the AC joint with the scapula & will follow the path of the clavicle.
				-The motions available at the SC joint include:
					Protraction: Has a concave surface moving on convex and has an anterior roll and anterior glide. 
					Elevation: Has a convex surface moving on a concave resulting in a superior roll and inferior glide.
					Axial rotation (spin).

		Scapulothoracic Joint
			-Recall that the scapulothoracic joint (STJ) is not a "true" synovial joint, rather it is formed between the scapula and the thorax, in which the scapula is suspended by the SC and AC joints and muscular attachments to the bony thorax.
				Scapular functions
					-The scapula serves as a platform for the humeral head to rest and move on via the glenoid cavity.
					-17 muscles attach to it.
					-Additionally, the scapula will transfer forces from the upper extremity to the trunk and vice versa.
					-Although highly variable, the average “resting position” of the scapula is about:
						10° - 20° of anterior tilt
						5 – 10° of upward rotation
						35° of internal rotation
					-Hand dominance does have an effect on the typical position of scapula, with the dominant side generally being more “downwardly rotated, depressed, and anteriorly tilted”. 
				Scapular support
					-The scapula is supported by the scapular muscles and the suspension of the scapula from the clavicle at the AC joint laterally. The clavicle receives support by the SC joint medially, which helps to strengthen the overall support system. 

		Scapulohumeral Rhythm 
			-The concept of scapulohumeral rhythm is the combination of glenohumeral and scapulothoracic movements. This generally occurs at a ratio of 2:1. This means for every 3 degrees of shoulder abduction, the GH joint abducts 2 degrees and the scapulothoracic joint upwardly rotates 1 degree.

			What actions must occur to complete shoulder abduction?
				-The clavicle must unlock to start the motion. The clavicle elevates and posteriorly rotates. Details of how that occurs are here: 
					-In phase I, the trapezius and serratus anterior create upward rotation of the scapula. Eventually, further upward rotation is halted as the AC joint prevents more motion by the coracoclavicular ligament. This forces the motion to occur at the SC joint where clavicular elevation occurs. 
					-In phase II, once clavicle elevation occurs at the SC joint, it will be eventually limited by the costoclavicular ligament. Where further scapular upward rotation causes clavicle posterior rotation, which will unlock the AC joint, and eventually allow for the rest of the upward rotation of the scapula and abduction of the shoulder.
				-The GH abduction motion occurs in combination with scapulothoracic upward rotation motion.

					-Shoulder instability refers to a condition where the head of the humerus (upper arm bone) does not stay properly centered in the glenoid fossa (shoulder socket). This can lead to:

						-This fear response is protective, as the patient anticipates potential instability or dislocation

			-Initiated in the resting position and progressed into the restricted ranges of motion based on the pain-restriction sequence and patient’s tolerance. Steps for Palpation
	Please review the steps for palpating the following structures. (See [MSK2_ListStructPalp_Shoulder])
		Long Head of Biceps Tendon
			To palpate the long head of the biceps tendon:
				Place the arm into neutral rotation.​
				Locate the greater and lesser tuberosity.​
				Externally rotate the arm and the lesser tuberosity becomes more palpable.​
				Internally rotate the arm the greater tuberosity becomes more prominent.
		Supraspinatus Tendon Γ('')
			To palpate the supraspinatus tendon:
				-Place the shoulder into adduction, extension and internal rotation by placing the patient’s hand behind the back.​ ¦Es la posició d'esposat
				-The greater tuberosity rotates anteriorly from under the coracoacromial arch.​
				Palpate the insertion of the suprapinatus tendon on the greater tuberosity just anterior to the acromion.
		Infraspinatus & Teres Minor Tendons Γ('')
			To palpate the infraspinatus tendon:
				The patient is prone on his or her elbows with the arms externally rotated. 
				The elbows are placed together forcing the shoulders into horizontal adduction.​
				-Follow the spine of the scapula to the acromion and palpate just inferior to the acromion to assess for pain or tenderness of the infraspinatus. 
			To palpate the teres minor tendon:
				Palpate one thumb breadth inferior to the infraspinatus.
				Alternative position for palpating infrapinatus and teres minor:​
					-The patient is seated with his or her arm placed in 90 degrees of flexion, horizontal adduction, and the elbow flexed to 90 degrees. 
						-The non-involved extremity is used to support the involved extremity at the elbow.  ​
						-The involved extremity is horizontally adducted and externally rotated.​
						-Follow the spine of the scapula to the acromion and palpates just inferior to the acromion for the infraspinatus and one thumb breadth below for the teres minor.
		Subscapularis Tendon & Fibers Γ('')
			To palpate the subscapularis tendon:
				-Position the patient supine with the arm at the side in neutral internal and external rotation ​
				-Find the coracoid and come 1” inferior and 1” lateral, you’ll be between the 2 tendons of the biceps at the edge the bicipetal groove​
				-Palpated deeply in the deltopectoral groove to palpate the tendon​
			To palpate the subscapularis fibers:​
				Abduct the upper extremity to expose the anterior surface of the scapula​
				Palpate through the axilla to the anterior surface of the scapula in an attempt to reach the subscapularis.

.Effect Exercises for Recruiting and Training Specific Shoulder Musculature
	Exercises to improve shoulder muscle function may be categorized into those that:
		Target rotator cuff muscles
			a.The purpose of these exercises is to recruit rotator cuff muscles in a manner that maximizes biomechanics and endurance during shoulder function
			b.Primary, this involves stabilization of the humeral head on the glenoid during overhead and demanding activities
			c.The goal is NOT to increase the force with which they contract – unless of course the patient is VERY weak
		Target scapular stabilizers
			a.Similarly, these are prescribed to maximize scapular mechanics and stability during function
			b.Again, the goal is proper recruitment and endurance rather than power

	(Γ)Why these test are considered red flags and what do we do if we find one of those on a patient?
		These tests are considered red flags because they screen for potentially serious conditions that could have severe consequences if left unaddressed. Here's why each test is a red flag and what to do if one is positive:
			RF1.Vertebral Artery Testing:
				- Why it's a red flag: Screens for vertebrobasilar insufficiency, which can lead to stroke.
				- If positive: Immediately stop the examination and refer the patient to a physician for further evaluation. This condition requires urgent medical attention.
			RF2.Sharp-Purser Test:
				- Why it's a red flag: Assesses for atlantoaxial instability, which can cause spinal cord compression.
				- If positive: Cease further cervical examination and refer the patient to a neurosurgeon or orthopedic spine specialist immediately. This condition poses a risk of severe neurological damage.
			RF3.Alar Ligament Stress Test (C2 Spinous Kick Test):
				- Why it's a red flag: Checks for upper cervical ligamentous instability, which can lead to neurological complications.
				- If positive: Stop the examination and refer the patient to a spine specialist for further imaging and assessment. This instability can compromise the integrity of the upper cervical spine.
			RF4.Shear Test (Lateral Shear Test):
				- Why it's a red flag: Evaluates for cervical spine instability, particularly in trauma cases.
				- If positive: Discontinue the examination and refer the patient for immediate medical evaluation, possibly including advanced imaging. This test suggests potential fracture or severe ligamentous injury.

			General protocol if any of these tests are positive:
				1. Immediately stop the examination to prevent further potential harm.
				2. Explain the findings to the patient in a calm, professional manner.
				3. Refer the patient to the appropriate medical specialist or emergency department.
				4. Document the findings thoroughly in the patient's record.
				5. Follow up with the patient and/or referring physician to ensure proper care was received.

			These tests are typically performed early in the examination process to rule out serious pathologies before proceeding with further assessment. The order presented is reasonable, as it progresses from assessing vascular issues to ligamentous stability of the upper cervical spine.

.Adhesive Capsulitis AKA Frozen Shoulder ('Frozen Shoulder')
	Overview
		-Adhesive capsulitis is a condition characterized by stiffness and pain in the shoulder joint that either comes on idiopathically (Primary) or after a long period of immobilization or disuse (Secondary)

					A decrease in synovial fluid volume

					Fracture​
					Arthritis​
					Neurologic conditions​
					Post surgically​
					Immobilization for any reason
			Primary Adhesive Capsulitis usually self-limiting passing through 4 stages:​
					Stage I – Pre-Freezing phase​
					Stage II – Freezing phase​ (Congelando)

.What is Osteoarthritis? ('Osteoarthritis.png')
	Overview
		-Osteoarthritis of the shoulder is a gradual wearing of the articular cartilage. As the glenohumeral joint surface continues to break down, the subchondral bone remodels itself, causing loss of convexity and congruity. This can result in pain and stiffness in the joint. The joint capsule also thickens, which can result in further loss of shoulder mobility.

.Conservative Treatment and Non-Surgical Care [6Hmb.3]

Proximal Humerus Fracture
	Overview ('Proximal humerus fracture.png')
		-Proximal humerus fractures may be displaced or non-displaced and may or may not require surgery. Once the healing fracture is stable enough, physical therapy may be of benefit to restore motion and function.

		-The physical exam is typically quite simple, focusing only on identifying possible deficits in:
			ROM
			Strength
			Capsular mobility
			Muscle Flexibility
			Daily functional tasks

		-Protocols will differ from patient to patient based on the type of fracture or surgery performed, but a common progression is as follows: [MSK2_ptSysClass_ProxHumerFract]
			Early Rehabilitation (0-2 weeks)
				Education
				Passive shoulder abduction and lateral rotation
				Pendulums
				Pain control
			Intermediate Rehabilitation (2-8 weeks)
				Supervised passive shoulder exercises in supine (flexion and lateral rotation)
				Light functional exercises without causing pain exacerbation
				Increase passive physiologic movements (not into resistance) to full range
				Proprioceptive exercises (closed chain and open chain)
			Late Rehabilitation (8 weeks and Beyond)
				Active exercise against gravity
				Isometric muscle work to strengthen rotator cuff muscles
				Reduce use of sling and encourage functional exercises
				Passive stretching if soft tissue contractions persist

AC Joint Pathology
	.Overview
		-Acromioclavicular joint separations (AKA: Separated Shoulder) occur exclusively via trauma. The most common mechanism is a fall in which the patient lands directly on the shoulder. This is very common in sports such as cycling, snowboarding, or tackle football.”
		-You will remember the classification of AC joint pathology from Anatomy. Review these below.

	.Rockwood Classification of AC Joint Pathology ('Rockwood Classification of AC Joint Pathology.png') (!!E.Grade & Doc)
		Type I
			Pathology:
				Sprained Acromioclavicular (AC) ligaments('')('AC CA CC ligaments'); normal Coracoclavicular ligaments (CC) ligaments('')
			Examination:
				Pain and swelling over the AC joint without a visible deformity
			Radiography:
				Normal
		Type II
			Pathology:
				Disruption of the AC ligaments; sprained CC ligaments
			Examination:
				Distal clavicle is unstable to horizontal stress; pain over the CC interspace
			Radiography:
				Widened AC joint and slight elevation of clavicle (<25%)
		Type III
			Pathology:
				Disruption of the AC and CC ligaments
			Examination:
				Distal clavicle is unstable to horizontal and vertical stress; reducible
			Radiography:
				Moderate elevation of the clavicle (25-100%)
		Type IV
			Pathology:
				Posterior displacement into or through the trapezius muscle
			Examination:
				Not reducible
			Radiography:
				Axillary view shows posterior displacement
		Type V
			Pathology:
				Rupture of the deltotrapezial fascia
			Examination:
				Clavicle palpable subcutaneously; not reducible
			Radiography:
				Distal clavicle is elevated to 300%)
		Type VI
			Pathology:
				Inferior displacement of the distal clavicle under the conjoined tendon
			Examination:
				Associated with rib fractures and neurovascular injury
			Radiography:
				Clavicle is in a subacromial or subcoracoid position

		.Common Subjective & Objective Findings
			Common Subjective Report for these Individuals is:  
				Local AC joint pain and a clear history of TRAUMA!!!
			Objective Findings are typically:
				-Presence of a “Step Off Sign” in which the clavicle is notably elevated in comparison to the acromion.
				-Painful and limited active and passive motion in most directions with the greatest limitation and pain provocation with horizontal adduction.
				-Painful palpation directly over the AC joint.

	Special Tests
		The following special tests may assist with assessment of AC joint pathologies:
			1.Cross Body Adduction Test
			2.O'Brien's Test
		These are demonstrated in the Shoulder Examination Module.

	Intervention Strategies for AC Joint Pathology vvv7a
		Rehabilitation for AC joint separation commonly progresses as described below based on patient tolerance:
			1.Initial immobilization
			2.PROM > AAROM > AROM
			3.Strengthen around involved area
			4.Strengthen shoulder muscles: Isometrics > Concentric ex arm at side > Concentric ex higher ranges

		-Stenosis: spaces narrow and create pressure
		-What is the difference between "structural stenosis" and "functional stenosis" for the shoulder?
			The terms "structural stenosis" and "functional stenosis" refer to different types of narrowing or constriction within the shoulder joint, with distinct underlying causes and implications:
				1.Structural Stenosis:
					This type of stenosis is caused by physical changes or abnormalities in the shoulder's anatomy. Examples include bone spurs, thickening of the joint capsule, or other structural deformities that physically narrow the space within the joint. Structural stenosis is often visible on imaging studies like X-rays or MRIs and may require interventions such as surgery to correct the anatomical issues.
				2.Functional Stenosis:
					In contrast, functional stenosis is related to the dynamic aspects of shoulder movement rather than fixed anatomical changes. It occurs when muscle imbalances, tendon tightness, or other soft tissue issues cause the shoulder structures to impinge or compress during certain movements. Functional stenosis may not be visible on static imaging and often improves with physical therapy aimed at restoring proper muscle balance and joint function.
			In summary, structural stenosis involves fixed anatomical changes, while functional stenosis involves dynamic issues related to muscle and soft tissue function.

Shoulder Girdle
	Without motion at one of the joints, you will have limited motion at the shoulder, generally showing up as reduced elevation
	Glenohumeral Motion
		The goal is to keep the humeral head centered on the glenoid.
	Rotator Cuff Muscles
		They produce some rotation motion
		but their most important job is to stabilize the humerus in position during overhead movements rather than rotating it through an arc motion

Subacromial Space
	Space between the humeral head inferiorly and the coracoacromial arch superiorly as the roof.
	It ouses many structures and is a site of frequent shoulder pathology.
	Soft tissues, such as the long head of biceps, rotator cuff, and bursa are located in the subacromial space.

MMTs - Rotator Cuff
	Internal Rotation and External Rotation testing positions
		Position 1: Prone with arm in 90 degrees of abduction
			Testing in this position is most valuable when:
				Significant weakness is suspected (can test against gravity)
				The patient does not have significant pain in the shoulder
				Neurological weakness is present
		Position 2: Seated/Standing with arm at side
			Testing in this position is most valuable when:
				Symptoms are severe or highly irritable
				Provocation of pain is of most interest
				Able to move against gravity is known or not needed
				Attempting to isolate infraspinatus (ER only)
		Position 3: Seated/Standing with arm in 90 degrees of abduction [MMTs_RC_Pos3]
			Testing in this position is most valuable when:
				Provocation of pain in overhead positions is of most interest
				Able to move against gravity is known or not needed
				Attempting to isolate teres minor (ER only)

Flexibility Assessment
	Assessment of flexibility in the upper extremity consists of determining if there is normal length of the following multiple-joint muscles
		Upper Arm
			Triceps brachii PROC_TricepsFlex
				-With the patient in sitting have the patient attempt to flex their shoulder with the elbow in full extension. Then passively flex the patient’s elbow.
				-If elbow flexion is more limited in this position than when measured  with the arm by the side decreased flexibility of the triceps is suspected
			Biceps brachii PROC_BicepsFlex
				With the patient in sitting or prone, fully extend the patient’s shoulder and then passively extend the elbow. 
				If elbow extension is more limited in this position than with the arm by the side, decreased flexibility of the biceps is suspected.
