                              ### Developing a Logical Framework for Choosing Vestibular and Ocular Motor Tests

                              When selecting tests or clusters of tests to perform on a patient, the goal is to efficiently and accurately diagnose the underlying cause of their symptoms. The choice of tests depends on the clinical presentation, history, and specific findings during the initial assessment. Below, I will explain the philosophy behind test selection, differentiate between tests based on their purpose, and provide practical examples through multiple cycles.

                              ---

                              ## Cycle 1: Philosophy Behind Test Selection

                              The philosophy of choosing one test (or cluster) over another revolves around:
                              1. Symptom Localization: Identifying whether the issue is central (brainstem, cerebellum) or peripheral (vestibular labyrinth, vestibular nerve).
                              2. Efficiency: Minimizing unnecessary testing while maximizing diagnostic yield.
                              3. Clinical Utility: Prioritizing tests that are most relevant to the patient’s chief complaint and history.
                              4. Progression: Starting with general screening tests and moving toward more specific assessments as needed.

                              ---

                              ### Key Questions to Guide Test Selection (!!E).InterviewBasics
                              - What is the patient’s primary symptom?
                                - Dizziness, vertigo, imbalance, visual disturbances, or hearing loss?
                              - Is the symptom acute or chronic?
                                - Acute presentations may require urgent differentiation between central and peripheral causes.
                              - Are there associated neurological signs?
                                - Skew deviation, cranial nerve palsies, or other findings suggest central pathology.
                              - Does the patient have a history of trauma, infection, or systemic disease?
                                - This helps guide suspicion toward structural or functional issues.

                              ---

                              ### Example 1: Acute Dizziness
                              A 60-year-old patient presents with acute dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. They also report mild imbalance but no hearing loss. 

                              #### Step 1: Rule Out Central Causes
                              - Perform the HINTS Exam (V9), which includes:
                                - Head Impulse Test (V8): To assess peripheral vestibular function.
                                - Nystagmus (V2): Look for direction-changing nystagmus.
                                - Test of Skew (V6): Check for skew deviation.
                              - Purpose: The HINTS exam is highly sensitive and specific for identifying posterior circulation strokes in patients with acute vestibular syndrome.

                              #### Step 2: Confirm Peripheral Vestibular Dysfunction
                              - If the HINTS exam suggests a peripheral cause:
                                - Perform Dix-Hallpike Test (BPPV.1) or Roll Test (HC-BPPV-PT.2) to assess for BPPV.
                              - Purpose: These positional tests confirm the presence of canalithiasis or cupulolithiasis.

                              ---

                              ## Cycle 2: Differentiating Between Tests Based on Purpose

                              Tests can be grouped by their purpose rather than their mechanics:

                              ### 1. Screening Tests for General Function
                              - Nine Quadrant Test (V1): Assesses ocular alignment and range of motion.
                              - Gaze Holding Nystagmus (V2): Detects gaze-evoked nystagmus, indicating central dysfunction.
                              - Smooth Pursuit (V3): Evaluates smooth tracking of moving objects; abnormalities suggest cerebellar involvement.

                              #### Practical Example: Chronic Imbalance
                              A 75-year-old patient reports chronic imbalance and occasional dizziness when walking. No acute onset or vertigo.

                              - Start with Nine Quadrant Test (V1) to rule out gross ocular misalignment.
                              - Follow with Smooth Pursuit (V3) and Saccades (V4) to assess cerebellar function.
                              - Purpose: These tests screen for subtle deficits in ocular motor control that could contribute to imbalance.

                              ---

                              ### 2. Diagnostic Tests for Specific Conditions
                              - Cover/Uncover Test (V6) and Cross-Cover Test (V7): Identify tropias (manifest) and phorias (latent) misalignments.
                              - Dynamic Visual Acuity Test (V11): Assesses vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) integrity during head movements.
                              - Head Shake Test (V10): Provokes nystagmus in patients with unilateral vestibular hypofunction.

                              #### Practical Example: Head Trauma
                              A 40-year-old patient had a recent head injury and now complains of dizziness and blurred vision during head movements.

                              - Use Dynamic Visual Acuity Test (V11) to evaluate VOR function.
                              - Perform Head Shake Test (V10) to provoke latent nystagmus.
                              - Purpose: These tests identify vestibular dysfunction caused by trauma.

                              ---

                              ### 3. Positional Tests for BPPV
                              - Dix-Hallpike (BPPV.1): Diagnoses posterior canal BPPV.
                              - Roll Test (HC-BPPV-PT.2): Diagnoses horizontal canal BPPV.

                              #### Practical Example: Episodic Vertigo
                              A 50-year-old patient experiences brief episodes of vertigo triggered by rolling over in bed.

                              - Perform Dix-Hallpike (BPPV.1) if symptoms occur with vertical head movements.
                              - If negative, perform Roll Test (HC-BPPV-PT.2) to check for horizontal canal involvement.
                              - Purpose: These tests localize the affected semicircular canal in BPPV.

                              ---

                              ## Cycle 3: Practical Examples Through Multiple Scenarios

                              ### Scenario 1: Concussion
                              A 25-year-old athlete sustained a concussion two weeks ago and now reports dizziness, headaches, and difficulty focusing during rapid head movements.

                              #### Test Selection:
                              1. VOMS (CT1–CT7): Assess vestibular and oculomotor function using:
                                 - Smooth pursuits (V3), saccades (V4), convergence (V5), and VOR exercises (CT1).
                              2. Dynamic Visual Acuity Test (V11): Evaluate VOR during head movements.
                              3. Balance Assessment: Use tools like the Berg Balance Scale or Functional Gait Assessment.

                              #### Rationale:
                              - Post-concussion symptoms often involve vestibular and oculomotor dysfunction.
                              - These tests help quantify impairments and guide rehabilitation.

                              ---

                              ### Scenario 2: Progressive Neurological Symptoms
                              A 65-year-old patient with a history of diabetes reports gradual onset of imbalance, double vision, and dizziness.

                              #### Test Selection:
                              1. HINTS Exam (V9): Rule out central causes like stroke or brainstem lesions.
                              2. Cover/Uncover Test (V6) and Cross-Cover Test (V7): Check for ocular misalignment.
                              3. Vibration-Induced Nystagmus (V12): Assess central vestibular pathways.

                              #### Rationale:
                              - Diabetes increases the risk of peripheral neuropathy and central nervous system complications.
                              - These tests differentiate between peripheral and central causes.

                              ---

                              ### Scenario 3: Hearing Loss
                              A 55-year-old patient reports hearing loss, tinnitus, and episodic vertigo lasting hours.

                              #### Test Selection:
                              1. Rinne and Weber Tests (V13 & V14): Assess conductive vs. sensorineural hearing loss.
                              2. Head Impulse Test (V8): Evaluate peripheral vestibular function.
                              3. Positional Testing (BPPV.1, HC-BPPV-PT.2): Rule out BPPV.

                              #### Rationale:
                              - Ménière’s disease or labyrinthitis may present with these symptoms.
                              - Audiometric and vestibular tests help confirm the diagnosis.

                              ---

                              ## Cycle 4: Summary of Logic and Decision-Making

                              ### Logic Flowchart
                              1. Acute vs. Chronic Presentation:
                                 - Acute: Prioritize HINTS Exam (V9) and positional tests (BPPV.1, HC-BPPV-PT.2).
                                 - Chronic: Screen with Nine Quadrant Test (V1), Smooth Pursuit (V3), and Saccades (V4).

                              2. Central vs. Peripheral Localization:
                                 - Central: Focus on HINTS Exam (V9), Vibration-Induced Nystagmus (V12), and Cover Tests (V6, V7).
                                 - Peripheral: Use Dix-Hallpike (BPPV.1), Roll Test (HC-BPPV-PT.2), and Head Impulse Test (V8).

                              3. Specific Complaints:
                                 - Dizziness: Dynamic Visual Acuity Test (V11), VOMS (CT1–CT7).
                                 - Hearing Loss: Rinne and Weber Tests (V13, V14).

                              ---

                              ### Final Answer
                              To choose the appropriate test(s):
                              1. Understand the purpose of each test—whether it screens, diagnoses, or localizes pathology.
                              2. Match the test to the patient’s presentation based on symptoms, history, and clinical findings.
                              3. Progress logically from general to specific assessments, prioritizing efficiency and relevance.

                              By applying this framework, you can systematically select the most appropriate tests for any given patient scenario.

                           ## ✅ V1: Nine Quadrant Test → Eye Movement Range of Motion
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Presence of spontaneous or gaze-evoked nystagmus
                              - Full range of motion in all directions (up, down, right, left, up-right, up-left, down-right, down-left)
                              - Smoothness and conjugacy of eye movements
                              - Any limitations, jerkiness, or drift back toward center

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Gaze-evoked nystagmus that increases with eccentric gaze suggests central pathology
                              - Nystagmus that decreases with visual fixation is more likely peripheral
                              - Restricted range of motion may indicate cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI)
                              - Asymmetric responses may suggest brainstem or cerebellar dysfunction

                              > From course content:  
                              > “The patient should look in primary gaze to the right, left, up, down, up into the right, up into the left, down into the right, down into the left... we should be assessing to see if the eyes are properly aligned, and if there are any nystagmus that we see in any of these positions.”

                           ## ✅ V2: Gaze Holding Nystagmus
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Ability to maintain steady gaze in eccentric positions
                              - Presence of nystagmus when maintaining gaze at 30° off-center (in up, down, right, or left gaze)

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Persistent nystagmus during gaze holding may indicate central lesions, especially cerebellar or brainstem involvement
                              - Normal response = no nystagmus or drift

                              > From course content:  
                              > “The physical therapist asks the patient to look in eccentric gaze for up to 30 seconds while the PT observes for presence of nystagmus.”

                           ## ✅ V3: Smooth Pursuit
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Eyes smoothly following a slowly moving target in horizontal and vertical planes
                              - No saccadic intrusions, jerking, or inability to follow the target

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Smooth pursuit deficits suggest cerebellar dysfunction
                              - Poor smooth pursuit can also occur in Parkinson’s disease, toxic/metabolic encephalopathy

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Smooth pursuit – ability to follow a slow-moving object across full range of vertical and horizontal eye movements.”

                           ## ✅ V4: Horizontal and Vertical Saccades
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Speed and accuracy of rapid eye movements between two targets
                              - Presence of hypometric, hypermetric, or slow saccades
                              - Presence of square wave jerks (small saccades interrupting fixation)

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Abnormal saccades may indicate central nervous system dysfunction, especially involving the frontal eye fields, superior colliculus, or cerebellum
                              - Slowed or inaccurate saccades seen in progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple sclerosis, stroke, or TBI

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Saccades are quick involuntary small movements of both eyes simultaneously... both eyes should accurately move together to the target.”

                           ## ✅ V5: Convergence
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Eyes turning inward as a target moves closer
                              - Ability to maintain single vision as target approaches nose
                              - Distance at which double vision occurs

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Convergence insufficiency common after concussion or traumatic brain injury
                              - Can contribute to visual disturbances and reading difficulties

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Convergence – the ability to bring both eyes inward toward the nose as a target moves closer.”

                           ## ✅ V6: Cover and Uncover Test (Cover Test)
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Eye movement when one eye is covered and then uncovered
                              - If the eye moves upward, downward, or laterally upon uncovering

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Detects manifest strabismus (tropia)
                              - Helps identify ocular misalignment or weakness in extraocular muscles
                              - May contribute to dizziness or diplopia

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Cover/uncover test: used to detect manifest strabismus (tropia).”

                           ## ✅ V7: Cross-Cover Test
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Eye movement when alternating cover between eyes
                              - Direction of eye movement when switching occlusion

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Identifies latent strabismus (phoria)
                              - May reveal binocular instability contributing to visual discomfort or motion sensitivity

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Cross-cover test: detects latent strabismus (phoria).”

                           ## ✅ V8: Head Impulse Test (Horizontal Canal)
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Patient maintains focus on target during rapid passive head rotation
                              - Presence of corrective saccade after head stops

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Corrective saccade indicates impaired VOR gain due to unilateral peripheral vestibular hypofunction
                              - Absence of corrective saccade = normal VOR function

                              > From course content:  
                              > “A positive HIT indicates reduced VOR gain and is associated with unilateral peripheral loss.”

                           ## ✅ V9: HINTS Exam (not explicitly labeled but relevant)
                              Γ See also the tests manually in this section

                              (Included here as it's often integrated with VOR testing)
                                 ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                                 - Head Impulse Test: presence of corrective saccade
                                 - Nystagmus type: direction-fixed vs. direction-changing
                                 - Test of Skew: vertical misalignment with cover-uncover

                                 ### 🧠 What It Means:
                                 - Direction-fixed nystagmus + positive HIT = peripheral lesion
                                 - Vertical nystagmus + skew deviation = central lesion
                                 - Used to differentiate central vs. peripheral causes of acute dizziness

                           ## ✅ V10: Head Shake Test (Head Shaking Nystagmus)
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Nystagmus after passive horizontal head shaking at ~2 Hz
                              - Direction and intensity of nystagmus
                              - Symptom provocation (dizziness, nausea)

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Horizontal nystagmus beating toward the healthy ear = unilateral vestibular hypofunction
                              - Vertical nystagmus post-head shake = possible central lesion
                              - A positive test supports diagnosis of vestibular asymmetry

                              > From course content:  
                              > “If vertical nystagmus is provoked post head shake, then this may be an indication of a central lesion.”

                           ## ✅ V11: Dynamic Visual Acuity Test (DVA)
                              ### 🔍 What You Are Looking For:
                              - Accuracy of reading an eye chart under static vs. dynamic head movement
                              - Number of lines lost during head movement (e.g., 2 Hz yaw plane shaking)

                              ### 🧠 What It Means:
                              - Loss of visual acuity during head motion = impaired VOR function
                              - Useful for quantifying functional impact of vestibular loss
                              - Guides rehabilitation goals and tracks progress

                              > From course content:  
                              > “Dynamic visual acuity test: clinical version assesses visual acuity during horizontal motion of the head.”

                        .V8.Head Impulse Test (Horizontal Canal)
                           1º.The patient is instructed to look at a target in midline.
                           2º.The PT slowly moves the patient’s head in the yaw plane about 15–20 degrees and then quickly moves the patient’s head to midline (the impulse) while the patient attempts to keep their eyes on the target.
                           3º.The head is rapidly stopped, and the PT checks to see if the patient has a “catch-up” saccade that will refixate their eyes on the target.

                           -The head can be moved away from midline for the impulse, but it is better to move toward the midline.
                           -The movement must be fully stopped before doing the next impulse.

                           -A normal VOR response to rapid passive head rotation is that the patient is able to fixate on the central target without saccades.
                           -An abnormal response occurs when head is rotated to the side of the vestibular lesion and there is an inability to maintain fixation during rotation of the head requiring a corrective saccade once the head has stopped moving.

                        .V13.Rinne test Γ(V13. & V14. go togerther)
                           discern sound transmitted through air versus bone and helps assess for unilateral hearing loss

                           1º.strike the fork inducing vibration
                           2º.The stem of the tuning fork is placed on the mastoid process of the patient
                           3º.The patient is then asked to cover their other ear with their hand
                           4º.The patient informs the PT when the sound can no longer be heard.
                           5º.The process is then repeated with the tines of the tuning fork held near the external auditory canal and the patient again informs the examiner when the sound can no longer be heard

                           Results:
                              Normal hearing is observed when:
                                 the vibrating tuning fork positioned near the ear is louder and lasts twice as long compared to when placed on the mastoid.
                              Abnormal:
                                 When the sound is at least equally loud or louder when the fork is placed on the mastoid compared to when it is held next to the ear.

                           .V14.Weber test Γ(V13. & V14. go togerther)
                              Used to assess for a unilateral hearing loss and to characterize conductive versus sensorineural loss.

                        ## 🧠 EYE MOVEMENT SYSTEMS: Overview mmm.ReferenceTable_AllAboutEyeMovements
                           Eye movements are essential for
                              visual stability,
                              tracking moving objects,
                              and shifting attention.
                           They are tightly controlled by multiple
                              brain regions,
                              cranial nerves,
                              and reflexive and voluntary pathways.

                           There are five main types of eye movements:
                              1. Saccades
                              2. Smooth Pursuit
                              3. Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR)
                              4. Optokinetic Nystagmus (OKN)
                              5. Vergence Movements

                        ## 1️⃣ Saccadic Eye Movements
                           ### 🔍 Definition:
                           - Rapid, ballistic movements that shift gaze from one point to another.
                           - Used to quickly refixate on new visual targets.

                           ### 📏 Speed:
                           - Fastest eye movement: 350–900°/sec

                           ### 🧭 Direction:
                           - Can be horizontal, vertical, or diagonal
                           - Occur in both conjugate (both eyes move same direction) and disconjugate (eyes move differently)

                           ### ⚙️ Control Pathway:
                           - Frontal Eye Fields (FEF) → Superior Colliculus → Brainstem Burst Neurons → Cranial Nerves III, IV, VI
                           - Basal Ganglia helps suppress unwanted saccades
                           - Cerebellum fine-tunes accuracy

                           ### 🧪 Clinical Meaning:
                           - Abnormal saccades (e.g., slow, hypometric, or dysmetric) suggest central nervous system lesions, such as cerebellar dysfunction or brainstem stroke
                           - Peripheral vestibular issues usually do not affect saccades

                        ## 2️⃣ Smooth Pursuit Eye Movements
                           ### 🔍 Definition:
                           - Slow, smooth tracking of moving visual targets
                           - Maintains target on fovea during motion

                           ### 🧭 Direction:
                           - Horizontal and vertical; requires intentional focus

                           ### 📏 Speed:
                           - Max speed: ~100°/sec

                           ### ⚙️ Control Pathway:
                           - Begins with visual cortex
                           - Information goes to middle temporal area (MT/V5) → frontal eye fields → dorsolateral pons → vestibulo-cerebellum → brainstem nuclei (III, IV, VI)

                           ### 🧪 Clinical Meaning:
                           - Impaired smooth pursuit may indicate cerebellar dysfunction, Parkinson’s disease, or toxic/metabolic encephalopathy
                           - Not typically affected in peripheral vestibular disorders

                        ## 3️⃣ Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR)
                           ### 🔍 Definition:
                           - Stabilizes vision during head movement
                           - Generates eye movement opposite to head movement

                           ### 🧭 Direction:
                           - Compensatory movement: opposite direction to head motion
                             - Head right → Eyes left
                             - Head up → Eyes down

                           ### ⚙️ Control Pathway:
                           - Vestibular apparatus → Vestibular nerve (CN VIII) → Vestibular nuclei → MLF → Cranial Nerve nuclei III, IV, VI

                           ### 🧪 Clinical Meaning:
                           - VOR integrity is tested via Head Impulse Test (HIT)
                           - Loss of VOR = peripheral vestibular loss (e.g., vestibular neuritis)
                           - Central causes can also alter VOR (e.g., cerebellar infarct)

                        ## 4️⃣ Optokinetic Nystagmus (OKN)
                           ### 🔍 Definition:
                           - Reflexive eye movement generated when large visual fields move across the retina
                           - Combines smooth pursuit and saccades

                           ### 🧭 Direction:
                           - Smooth phase follows moving stimulus; fast phase resets gaze

                           ### ⚙️ Control Pathway:
                           - Visual input → Visual cortex → MT/V5 → Parietal cortex → Brainstem centers

                           ### 🧪 Clinical Meaning:
                           - Absent OKN suggests visual pathway lesion or cortical blindness
                           - Helps assess visual processing and integration with eye movements

                        ## 5️⃣ Vergence Eye Movements
                           ### 🔍 Definition:
                           - Disconjugate eye movements used to align eyes on near or far targets

                           ### 🧭 Types:
                           - Convergence: eyes turn inward (near focus)
                           - Divergence: eyes turn outward (far focus)

                           ### ⚙️ Control Pathway:
                           - Triggered by retinal disparity
                           - Involves visual cortex, frontal eye fields, and midbrain
                           - Convergence center located in rostral midbrain

                           ### 🧪 Clinical Meaning:
                           - Convergence insufficiency common in traumatic brain injury (TBI)
                           - Useful in assessing concussion symptoms and visual-vestibular integration

                        ## 🧬 NEUROANATOMY INVOLVED IN EYE MOVEMENT CONTROL
                           | Structure                            | Role in Eye Movement |
                           |--------------------------------------|----------------------|
                           | Frontal Eye Fields (FEF)             | Initiates voluntary saccades |
                           | Superior Colliculus                  | Coordinates reflexive saccades |
                           | Parieto-occipital cortex             | Processes visual motion for smooth pursuit |
                           | Dorsolateral Pons                    | Integrates signals for smooth pursuit |
                           | Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF) | Coordinates conjugate horizontal gaze |
                           | Cerebellum (flocculonodular lobe)    | Modulates VOR and smooth pursuit accuracy |
                           | Vestibular nuclei                    | Integrates vestibular info to control VOR |
                           | Brainstem burst neurons              | Generate rapid saccadic commands |
                           | Cranial Nerves III, IV, VI           | Innervate extraocular muscles |

                        ## 🧪 CLINICAL INTERPRETATION OF ABNORMAL EYE MOVEMENTS
                           | Finding                                                  | Likely Cause |
                           |----------------------------------------------------------|---------------|
                           | Slow or hypometric saccades                              | Cerebellar lesion, Parkinson's |
                           | Square-wave jerks (small saccades interrupting fixation) | Brainstem or cerebellar dysfunction |
                           | Horizontal gaze palsy                                    | Pontine lesion (e.g., stroke) |
                           | Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)                       | Lesion in MLF (common in MS) |
                           | Downbeat nystagmus                                       | Cerebellar lesion (especially flocculus) |
                           | Upbeat nystagmus                                         | Brainstem or medullary lesion |
                           | Gaze-evoked nystagmus                                    | Central lesion (if persistent), normal in some people |
                           | Direction-fixed nystagmus                                | Central pathology |
                           | Spontaneous nystagmus without stimulus                   | Peripheral or central vestibular dysfunction |

                        ## 🧭 TYPES OF NYSTAGMUS & WHAT THEY MEAN
                           | Type of Nystagmus              | Description                    | Clinical Significance                  |
                           |--------------------------------|--------------------------------|------------------------|
                           | Spontaneous nystagmus          | Occurs without stimulus        | Peripheral or central vestibular issue |
                           | Positional nystagmus           | Triggered by head position     | BPPV if transient and geotropic |
                           | Gaze-evoked nystagmus          | Occurs at extreme gaze         | Central if persistent |
                           | Downbeat nystagmus             | Fast phase downward            | Cerebellar pathology |
                           | Upbeat nystagmus               | Fast phase upward              | Brainstem or pontine lesion |
                           | Rotational/torsional nystagmus | Spiral-like eye movement       | Indicates vestibular involvement |
                           | Optokinetic nystagmus absence  | No response to moving stripes  | Visual pathway or cortical lesion |
                           | End-point nystagmus            | Mild nystagmus at extreme gaze | Normal variant |

                        ## 🧩 KEY CONCEPTS FROM THE COURSE MATERIAL

                           From your course materials:

                           - The medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) coordinates conjugate horizontal gaze between both eyes.
                           - The vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) keeps images stable during head motion.
                           - The cerebellum and vestibular nuclei integrate sensory inputs to modulate eye movements.
                           - Voluntary saccades are driven by the frontal eye fields, while reflexive saccades originate from the superior colliculus.
                           - Smooth pursuit involves temporal and frontal eye fields, and is modulated by the vestibulo-cerebellum.
                           - Vergence movements are triggered by retinal disparity and involve the midbrain convergence center.
