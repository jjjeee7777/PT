.Introduction
	In this module, you will learn how to screen for red flag conditions that may initially present as common pathologies affecting the lumbar and sacral spines and a systematic examination process for patients presenting with low back pain.

	This content includes performance of common tests and measures for the spine, a description of patho-anatomical spinal conditions, as well as an introduction to the treatment-based classification approach to managing low back pain. 

	Learning Objectives
		After completing this module, you should be able to:

		Identify red flag conditions that may present as common conditions affecting the lumbar and sacral spines. 
		Conduct a thorough patient history for patients with low back pain. 
		Perform a physical examination for patients with spinal conditions. 
		Summarize the treatment-based classification approach to management of low back pain.

.1.2. Screening for Red Flag Conditions
	-There are a number of serious or life-threatening conditions that may mask as mechanical low back pain.
	-Therefore, one of the first considerations when evaluating a patient with low back pain is gathering information to help screen for the presence of red flag conditions.  
	-Standard signs of red flag conditions for patients presenting to physical therapy often include:
		Fever/chills/sweats
		Unexplained weight loss
		Fatigue/malaise 
		Nausea and vomiting 
		Nighttime pain 
		``(Γ)1.2.I::What underlaying conditions could lye behind these symptoms?
	-While these are always important to consider, the remainder of this lesson will describe more specific considerations for red flag conditions that may present as typical mechanical low back pain.
	---------------------------------------------
	(1.2.a.)Situational Warning Signs in Patients with Low Back Pain 
		When a physical therapist encounters any of the following scenarios, they should consider the following: 
			1.History of Malignancy → screened for secondary malignant deposits
				-A patient who presents with a backache and/or having a history of a malignancy during the previous two years, must be assumed to have and screened for secondary malignant deposits in the spine until proven otherwise.
			2.Onset of Back Pain Late in Life → more likely to have osteoporosis
				-An elderly individual without any previous history of back symptoms is more likely to have osteoporosis than some simple mechanical fault.
				-The clinician should therefore be certain the patient has been screened appropriately. 
			3.Continuous Pain Unrelated to Posture → Γ Systemic disease or internal orgam
				-Unlikely to be mechanical in origin.
			4.Loss of Strength too Widespread to be Accountable by a Single Nerve Root Lesion
				-Often suggests a neurological disease.
			5.Loss of Sphincter Control
				-Never due to simple mechanical causes.
			``(Γ)1.2.II::What is due then?

.1.3. Possible Signs of Systemic Disease
	These characteristics may be indicative of the presence of a systemic condition: 
		-Very intense pain which requires morphine for more than 48 hours may indicate the presence of a serious systemic disease.
		-Severe pain, deformity, and muscle spasm in areas of the spine other than lower cervical and lower lumbar should also arouse suspicion of systemic disease.
		-Constitutional signs Γ(a general, non-specific symptom that can affect many different body systems) which accompany back pain like pyrexia Γ(fever), loss of weight, malaise, and excessive weakness, suggest disease.
	Other Possible Red Flag Signs of inflammatory/systemic disease may include:
		Body Temperature
			> 100° F
		Blood Pressure
			> 160/95 mmHg
		Resting Pulse
			> 100 beats per minute (BPM)
		Resting Respiration
			> 25 breaths per minute
		Discoloration
			Example: jaundice
	(1.3.a)Ruling out Cancer as a Cause of Low Back Pain (LBP)
		Cancer as a cause of LBP can be ruled out with 100% sensitivity (See [MSK1_Spin&Snout]) from the history and intervention when: 
			Age is less than 50 y-o
			No prior history of cancer
			No unexplained weight loss
			Conservative treatment is successful
			('Cancer as a cause of back pain.pdf')

1.5. Evaluation of Red Flag Conditions
	But how much weight should one give to a single red flag finding?
		The following study provides some perspective on this: ('Arthritis Rheumatism spinal pathology.pdf')
			-Here is a summary of their findings:
				-In this study, a cohort of 1172 patients w/ acute LBP were asked 25 questions used to identify “red flag” conditions.
				-Although 80% of these individuals had at least 1 RF finding, < 1% (11) actually had any form of “serious pathology” such as spinal fracture, cancer, cauda equina syndrome, or an inflammatory disorder.
				-With multiple red flags, the probability increased to 14% (2 red flag answers) and 68% with 3 red flag answers.
			-The authors concluded that one red flag answer was not considerably predictive, but multiple (especially 3 or more) considerably increased the odds.

.1.8. Physical Examination of the Lumbar Spine
	-The following sections will review components of physical examination of the lumbar spine, which may include:
		Observation/Inspection 
		Lower Quarter Screening 
		Active Range of Motion

	General Guidelines for the Physical Exam
		The following factors should guide how you conduct your physical exam:
			Establish baseline symptoms before you start movement testing. 
			Seek (in most cases) to reproduce a comparable sign. 
				-Movement that reproduces patient’s familiar symptom. 
				-The ability to identify a comparable sign associated with the patient’s familiar symptoms has been associated with a better prognosis.
				-Multiple symptoms may = multiple comparable signs.
			Plan the vigor of your assessment through tests and measures based on the patient’s identified SINSS.

.1.9. Observation/Inspection of the Lumbar Spine
	When performing observation and inspection specific to the lumbar spine:
		The lumbar region should be directly observable. 
		Have the patient remove overlying clothing as able. 
		Pay special attention to the patient’s level of apparent discomfort, ability to move in/out of chair for examination, etc. 
			This may provide further information about SINSS.
		Look for antalgic positions such as: 
			An inability to stand erect. 
			Unwillingness or avoidance of weight-bearing to a particular side. 
			A lateral shift of the trunk relative to pelvis.
	Clinical note: ('lateral shift of the trunk relative to pelvis')
		-A lateral shift, pictured below, occurs when the patient is reflexively positioned with the upper trunk notably shifted so it is no longer aligned with the lower trunk. The image on the right shows a lateral shift to the right, while the image on the left shows a more subtle shift to the left.
	A reminder when drawing conclusions about a patient’s posture
		Static posture/self-selected posture is not always well-correlated with LBP.
		However, any posture can become painful over time.
		Do not assume that a patient’s self-selected posture is associated with decreased symptoms.
			Patients will sometimes persist with a posture even though it increases pain.

.1.10. Lower Quarter Screening (LQS) [MSK2_Imm3A_LQS]
	When appropriate, perform a lower quarter screening (LQS).
		A LQS is performed when neurological involvement is SUSPECTED or POSSIBLE but not certain.
			Standard components of the LQS include:
				Sensation/Dermatome Testing 
					Sensory innervation of spinal nerve roots.
				Myotome Testing
					Motor innervation of spinal nerve roots. 
				Deep Tendon Reflexes
					Assessment of the involuntary reflex loop between musculotendinous structures and the spinal cord.
		If neurological involvement is CERTAIN then screening is omitted and detailed neuromuscular exam is performed.
	('02 Mod1 LowerQuarterScreen.mp4')
		PLEASE NOTE:
			-The following video describes sensation screening using both Key ASIA (American Spinal Cord Association) Points AND sensation testing using longer strokes covering representative areas within each dermatome. Circumstances under which each are appropriate will be discussed further during live sessions but as we learned in MSK 1 (See [MSK1_LQS]), it is typically more useful to screen sensation using the longer representative strokes within each dermatome. 
		As a review, this involves:
			-Performing 6-8" Γ(15.24 to 20.32 cm) strokes in similar areas on right and left sides of the body
			-Performing multiple strokes within each dermatome, with the goal being to assess sensation of the skin in the MAJORITY of the dermatome
				-i.e., you do not need to assess every millimeter of skin - just enough to be sure you are not missing any large areas)
			-With the patient's eyes closed, asking him/her if they note any differences in sensation from side to side

.1.11.1. Variations of Lumbar Motion Testing
	-Variations of lumbar motion testing include:
		1.Quadrant Testing
		2.Repeated Movement Testing
		3.Passive Lumbar Motion Testing (See [MSK2_1.12.Passive Lumbar Motion Testing])
		4.Accessory Lumbar Motion Testing (See ¿?)
	-Review the following information for each of the variation tests. Please note that passive and accessory lumbar motion tests will be covered on the next page. 
		1.Quadrant Testing (Combined Movement Testing) ('combined movement testing of the lumbar spine.png')
			-Quadrant testing, also known as combined movement testing of the lumbar spine, is another way to attempt to provoke the patient’s symptoms. This is especially helpful when the patient’s symptoms are not provoked with straight plane movements of flexion, extension and side bend. 
				-Common combined movements include either extension combined with side bend left or right (pictured below) or flexion combined with side bending​. 
			-Variations on interpretation:
				-SB & Ext same side painful: Possible tissue/joint compression? 
				-SB / Ext opposite side painful: Possible tissue stretch? 
				-Most accurate interpretation? Simply another way to provoke sxs in addition to cardinal plane movement or when cardinal plane motion is normal.
		2.Repeated Movement Testing ('2024-05-07 12_05_51-StrokesPlus')
			-Repeated movement testing is most appropriate for patients who report a pattern of change or variability in the location of their symptoms.
				Peripheralization: Pain moving further away from the spine
				Centralization: Pain moving closer to the spine
					Note that intensity of pain is not the key here in identifying a movement preference. 
			Repeated Movement (RM) Assessment 
				-McKenzie examination protocol calls for repeated movements of multiple reps to assess for worsening/peripheralization of symptoms. 
			Considerations for RM testing: 
					Let history, SINSS, and rest of exam guide: 
						Number of reps/sets
						Position in which you test
						Centralization typically more important to identify than peripheralization
			Repeated movement testing variations 
				The following are examples of possible positions in which to test repeated extension: 
					Prone lying (positioning) 
					Prone on elbows 
					Prone on hands 
					Extension in plantigrade 
					Extension in standing (partial) 
					Extension in standing (full) 
	Knowledge Check
		-Repeated movement testing in a given direction should be implemented into the treatment plan when that movement causes: 
			More pain
			Less pain
			Peripheralization of symptoms 
			Centralization of symptoms

			Centralization of symptoms
				Repeated movement should be implemented into the treatment plan when the movement causes symptoms to be focused closer to the spine (centralization) and not when they radiate (peripheralization). Note that decreased pain is not necessarily the goal, but rather to see which movements reduce peripheralization.

				(Γ)-------------------------------------------------------
				"SnOut" and "SpPin" are mnemonics used in medicine to describe the properties of diagnostic tests:

				1. "SnOut": (¦Si vullc que siga sensible, xo no està)
				   - "SnOut" stands for "Sensitive, Negative rules Out." 
				   - This means that a test with high sensitivity (ability to correctly identify those with the condition) and a negative result effectively rules out the presence of the condition.
				   - In other words, if the test accurately detects the absence of the condition when it's truly not there, it's considered helpful for ruling out the condition.
				   - In the context of the Straight Leg Raise (SLR) test for lumbar herniated nucleus pulposus (HNP) with radiculopathy, "SnOut" suggests that SLR, being sensitive (picking up most cases of the condition), is useful for ruling out lumbar HNP with radiculopathy when the test result is negative.

				2. "SpPin":
				   - "SpPin" stands for "Specific, Positive rules In." (¦Especialment tú no, xo ahí estàs)
				   - This means that a test with high specificity (ability to correctly identify those without the condition) and a positive result effectively confirms the presence of the condition.
				   - In other words, if the test accurately detects the presence of the condition when it's truly there, it's considered helpful for confirming the condition.
				   - In the context of the SLR test, "SpPin" suggests that a cross-positive SLR (reproduction of contralateral leg pain) is specific (unlikely to be positive in the absence of the condition) and helpful for confirming lumbar HNP with radiculopathy when the test result is positive.

.1.21. The Treatment-Based Classification (TBC) System
	-This system evolved as a response to the failures of the patho-anatomical model.
	-Treatment-Based Classification (TBC) is a model whereby the clinician makes treatment decisions based on the patient's clinical presentation.
	-The primary purpose of the TBC approach is to identify baseline patient characteristics that predict responsiveness to varying treatment strategies. 
	-The TBC for the spine is not without its critics, however. Reasons for this include:
		Some patients are difficult to classify.
			-The overall reliability of an early system is 75.5% (Henry et al 2012) – which is not bad, but not high either. 
		It may not attend enough to psychosocial factors.
		There is conflicting research on its effectiveness.
	-The next module will outline the evolution of various TBCs for the spine in more detail.

.2.2. Treatment-Based Classification Systems for Management of Spinal Conditions: The Pioneers
	-Contemporary versions of treatment-based classification systems (TBCs) are to some extent, based on earlier models. Some aspects of these models have been retained in present versions, while others have been modified or expanded upon. 
	-But the very first TBC for low back pain, was developed by individuals affiliated with a department within an institution that should be very familiar. 
	-The authors of the first research article describing classification systems for patients with low back pain were Anthony Delitto, Richard Bowling, and Richard Erhard, all of whom had ties to the Physical Therapy Department at the University of Pittsburgh.

.2.5. Why The Focus on Classification of Patients with Low Back Pain?
	-Before we outline the components of this model, it is worthwhile to consider why focus on classification systems for low back pain are important. 
	-The answer to this question is based in historical research analyzing the effectiveness of interventions for reducing low back pain.  
	-Prior to the evolution of TBCs, the majority of research has reached few conclusions about which approaches are effective for reducing pain and disability. 
	-The opening paragraph from the article ('Treatment-Based Classification System for Low Back Pain.pdf') describes the revision and update for TBCs provides additional perspective on this matter:
		"Despite the plethora of research on low back pain (LBP),
		clinical trials have not provided conclusive evidence
		supporting the superiority of any particular intervention.
		This gap is often attributed to the fact that the design of
		most clinical trials includes delivery of a single intervention
		to a heterogeneous group of patients with LBP. This
		heterogeneity, combined with wide inclusion criteria, tends
		to dilute the treatment effect. In order to optimize the
		treatment effect, patients with LBP should be classified
		into homogeneous subgroups and matched to a specific
		treatment. Subgroup-matched treatment approaches have
		been shown to result in improved outcomes compared with
		non-matched alternative methods."
			Airwaily et al. (2016)

				Diluted Treatment Effect due to Heterogeneity
					We will explore these ideas in greater detail in a synchronous session but when it comes to the treatment of low back pain: 
						-Patients with low back pain present with wide variety of signs and symptoms. 
						-These signs/symptoms are the result of impairment of many different anatomical sources. 
						-Some patients respond very well to certain treatments for low back pain while others do not.

	.Question 10
	A physical therapist should conclude that a prone instability test is positive if posterior to anterior glides are:
	    Painful in prone and with the lower extremities elevated
	    With the lower extremities elevated but not in prone
	    Painful in prone but less painful with the lower extremities elevated
	    Neither painful in prone nor with the lower extremities elevated
	    Hypermobile at multiple lumbar levels regardless of whether or not they reproduce pain

	    Rationale: The prone instability test is used to assess the presence of lumbar spine instability. During the test, the patient lies prone on the examination table with their torso on the table and legs off the edge, touching the floor. The examiner performs posterior to anterior (P-A) glides on the lumbar vertebrae, noting any pain response. The patient is then asked to lift their legs off the floor (engaging the lumbar stabilizers), and the P-A glides are repeated.

This suggests that the lumbar pain is related to segmental instability, which is stabilized when the muscles are activated.

3.2. Manual Therapy for the Spine
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

Manual therapy techniques are commonly used to reduce impairments in patients with low back pain.

In preparation for this lesson, consider the following questions:
	What are the effects of manual therapy?
	How is it of benefit for patients with low back pain?

The effects of manual therapy for the spine can be described in terms of neurophysiological, biomechanical, and psychological effects.
	.Neurophysiological Effects
		-Are those provided by stimulation of neuroreceptors in joint, cutaneous, and muscle tissue. Stimulation of these structures in the periphery can set in motion neural pathways that activate the body’s internal mechanisms of relieving pain such as the "Gate Control Theory of Pain" to an external site. ('31 Mod2 Gate Control Theory of Pain.mp4')
		-While these effects are typically temporary they can allow patients to achieve long term benefits by allowing the performance of activity and exercise that would otherwise not be possible.
		-Furthermore, temporary disruption of constant pain, can produce longer-lasting effects by interrupting the pain-spasm-pain cycle (shown below) in which the presence of pain results in muscle spasm which sets in motion events that cause greater levels of pain.

		('Mod 2_pain stress.png')
			Pain Stress ↓
				Muscle Tension
					Decrease in Circulation
						Increase in Pain
							Even More Tension
								Even Less Circulation
									More Pain ↑

	.Biomechanical Effects ('Mod 2_lumbar instability.png')
		-Mechanical deformation of connective tissues can result in increased mobility in joints and other tissues that were previously hypomobile. 
		-Regaining lost movement, in turn, may produce gains in function but also has potential to relieve stress on adjacent joints and tissues that were receiving increased levels of mechanical stress during movement 
		-You may recall discussion of the "principle of regional interdependence" where lack of movement in one body region results in increased stress and movement in adjacent structures as stress from movement is transferred to these, more mobile areas. 
			For example, if the L4-L5 vertebral segment is hypomobile, additional stress will be placed on the L3-L4 and/or L4-L5 vertebral segments as the spine attempts to achieve normal movement during function.

	.Psychological Effects
		Finally, we must acknowledge that there can be psychological benefits that result from manual therapy approaches.
			-These may simply be due to a placebo effect resulting from the patient perceiving that something beneficial is occurring.  
			-However, the benefits of human touch are not fully known and the possibility exists that physical contact can produce advantages that are not well-understood.

-Nonetheless, evidence supports the benefit of manual therapy in addition to exercise and other approaches for patients with low back pain.

Joint Mobilization and Other Non-Thrust Techniques ('Joint mobilization - Grades') [MSK2_JointMob01]
	-Joint mobilization is defined as gentle type of manual therapy during which the physical therapist performs sustained rhythmic passive accessory movements that target a specific synovial articulation. 3.4. Lumbar Manipulation: Rationale and Performance
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

Another tool physical therapists have at their disposal to decrease pain and enhance movement is spinal manipulation 
	○	Lumbar manipulation sometimes referred to as "grade V mobilization" is characterized by use of a high velocity (but low amplitude) thrust. Joint mobilizations, in contrast, are performed at a slower velocity
	○	How does spinal manipulation benefit patients?
		■	While some clinical philosophies state that spinal manipulation can be used to realign the spine when it is “out of alignment”, there is no credible evidence to support this theory. Research suggests that manipulation techniques do not reposition vertebral segments in any way.
			●	However studies do support that spinal manipulation can facilitate more effective movement 
	○	So how does spinal manipulation produce benefits?
		■	Prevailing theory stated
			●	Stimulates neuroreceptors within and surrounding vertebral segments
			●	This facilitates a reflex relaxation in surrounding spinal musculature 
			●	Which in turn, can result in more freely moving spinal segments and less pain during function
		Whether or not this is the exact mechanism, evidence does substantiate short-term benefits associated with spinal manipulation.

3.5. Manipulation: Additional Considerations
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

-Before we wrap up this lesson, here are a couple of important considerations when comparing high velocity thrust manipulation to non-thrust joint mobilization.
	-2.Research suggests that production of a cavitation (audible pop) during high velocity thrust manipulation is not necessary to produce positive effects. In other words, clinicians should not be focused on if a cavitation is achieved – and should inform their patients of this.
	-1.Most research comparing thrust manipulation to non-thrust mobilization does not find one to be superior to the other for decreasing pain and increasing mobility. Both are beneficial interventions and the physical therapist should utilize whichever is best tolerated by the patient and/or he/she feels comfortable performing.

Contraindications - When NOT to do manipulation
	Lastly, there are certain contraindications for spinal manual therapy/manipulation. 
		These include:
			Inflammatory arthritis
			Malignancy
			Bone disease
			Neurological Involvement
				Specifically, spasticity that prevents passive movement
			Fracture or congenital bone deformities
				In the area of focus
			Hypermobility
			Connective tissue disorders that predispose the patient to an increased risk of tissue damage
			Pain with pre-mobilization or manipulation positions
				Most mobilizations should be comfortable to tolerable
				A manipulation should NEVER be painful

.Kinesthetic Training Procedures
	Now, to elaborate a bit more on the idea of kinesthetic awareness and “proper” spinal position.
		-There is a wide range of recommendations regarding what is the “best” position in which to place your spine during function and exercise.
		-Here are a couple of the most commonly quoted recommendations for spinal positioning during exercise:
			“In a neutral position, half-way between anterior and posterior tilt.”
			“In full posterior tilt, so as to fully engage the abdominal positions.”
	However, the goal of creating kinesthetic awareness is:
	    To develop proprioceptive awareness of beneficial posture, positioning, and safe movement.

	    .In these terms, “proper” positioning means awareness of:
			●	Asymptomatic spinal positions
			●	Proper complementary posture in other regions(scapula, c-spine)
			●	Safe spinal motion and range
			●	The effects of extremity motion on spine
			●	All of the above in variety of positions 

.Creating Awareness of Spinal Position: Early Stages of Training
	-During initial stages of training (especially during acute episodes), it may be appropriate to help the patient identify and establish a relatively static position of comfort. This position of comfort has been given many different names including "functional position", "neutral position", "asymptomatic position", "safe position", etc. The exact definition of such terms is quite controversial, but here are a few interpretations:
		-"The neutral position is the alignment in which their spine tolerates mechanical forces best. This position can vary from person to person and, in some cases, can vary over time in an individual depending on changes in pathology." Dean Farwood, P.T.
		-"Neutral spine is a position or range of movement defined by the patient's symptoms, pathology, and current musculoskeletal restrictions. It is a position in which a vertical force exerted through the spine allows equal weight transference to the weight bearing surfaces." Robin Robison, P.T.
	-But one interpretation that makes considerable sense, is from Dennis Morgan who states:
		-The spine has a range of optimal positions in which it functions most efficiently, and these positions vary with its physiologic status and the stresses it must withstand. The functional position is the most stable and asymptomatic position of the spine for the task at hand. Dennis Morgan, P.T., D.C
	-Creating Awareness of Spinal Positioning
		-Based on the above quotes, it can be seen that different loads are placed on the spine in various positions.  These also vary from individual to individual. It is therefore important to create awareness of this concept under a variety of circumstances.
		-Based on this interpretation, creating awareness of achieving the most beneficial position will:
			Maximize tolerance of the exercise or activity.
			Because pain and signs of inflammation are avoided, minimize the chance for reinjury.
	-Review of Knowledge Check (!!Q)
		-Therefore, the best answer for the prior knowledge check question would be: the pelvic position between anterior and posterior tilt that feels most comfortable.
			-It should be noted, that according to this philosophy, the most asymptomatic position CAN VARY GREATLY:
				-From individual to individual
				-Based on the specific task, exercise or body position of the patient during performance
				-Within the same patient for the same task during DIFFERENT PHASES OF THE RECOVERY PROCESS.
				-In other words, what is the most comfortable position during a certain exercise acutely may change as the patient reaches the subacute stage.
	Progression of Training
		-Lastly and very importantly, sustaining a singular, static position should only be utilized early in the recovery process for the purpose of minimizing production of exacerbating symptoms.
			-As the patient recovers, progression of this concept should include:
				■	Exploring safe and asymptomatic pelvic and spinal ranges
				■	Allowing limited spinal and pelvic movement within these ranges during exercise and function
				■	Eventually, progressing to near full spinal and pelvic movement avoiding only the symptomatic extremes of motion

			-Considering that the end goal is to have the stabilizing contraction carry over into functional activities, the practicality of achieving this with other cues that elicit a much more intense effort and contraction such as “pretend like you are going to punched in the stomach” or are “putting on tight jeans”.
			-In other words, is it realistic to expect patients to sustain these types of abdominal contractions while performing functional activities like walking or housework over several minutes?

		-To summarize, a physical therapist following this process would implement these principles while teaching performance of an exercise or activity as follows:
			-While positioned as required for the exercise or task (supine, quadruped, kneeling, standing, etc.) have the patient explore pelvic range between anterior and posterior to tilt.
			-Identify and assume the most comfortable position within this range (note that this position may be biased towards anterior or posterior tilt or somewhere the middle).
			-While maintaining this position, contract that transversus abdominis by gently drawing in the lower abdomen just below the naval.
			-Begin performance of the task while maintaining the position and contraction.

		Example
			If the patient were learning to perform a bridging exercise, they should be instructed to do as follows:
				-Move the pelvis forward and back until you find the most comfortable spot in which you would feel comfortable staying for a while.
				-Hold that position and gently pull your lower belly in a small amount.
				-Maintain the position of the spine and the abdominal contraction while lifting the bottom from the floor.

3.17. Progression of Stabilization Exercises
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

General Principles for Altering Difficulty of Stabilization Exercises
	-As with other forms of exercises, there are a number of general approaches that may be used to increase or decrease the challenge of stabilization exercises. Some of these include:
		●	-Change lever arm
			○	In this bridging exercise, changing the lever arm can be accomplished by moving the ball either closer or further from the patient’s bottom.
		●	-Change Base of support
			○	Using or not using an exercise ball
			○	Moving supportive extremities further or less apart
			○	Performing exercises in maximally supported vs less-supported positions such as progressing from
				■	Supine to hook lying to bridging to quadruped to kneeling
		●	-Change Speed 
		●	-Add resistance 
		●	-Increase/Decrease the complexity of the movement. With stabilization exercises, this can mean:
			○	Movement in straight vs. diagonal planes
			○	Superimposed movement of single vs. multiple extremities
		-Modifying the Complexity of Superimposed Movements
			-Modifying the complexity of superimposed movements is often accomplished by adding various combinations of upper and lower extremity movements. The typical progression from less to more challenging would be superimposed movements of:
				Only the upper extremities
				Only the lower extremities
				Alternating upper and lower extremity movements (i.e., first move arms, then move legs)
				Simultaneous upper and lower extremity movements (moving arms and legs at the same time)
		-Clinical note: As with all exercise progression, the starting point for stabilization exercise should strongly also consider severity, irritability, nature, stability, and stage of the patient’s symptoms.

	Stabilization Exercise Progression for a 66-Year-Old Male with Lumbar Stenosis and Herniated Discs

	Initial Position: Supine

	1. Starting Exercise: Drawing-In Maneuver (Transverse Abdominis Activation)
	   - Position: Supine, knees bent (hook-lying position), feet flat on the floor.
	   - Instructions: 
	     - Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat on the floor.
	     - Place your fingers just inside the bony prominences at the front of your hips.
	     - Gently draw your lower abdomen in towards your spine, trying to contract your transverse abdominis without moving your pelvis or upper body.
	     - Hold this contraction for 5-10 seconds, breathing normally.
	     - Repeat for 10-15 repetitions.

	2. Progression Within the Same Position: Single-Leg Marches
	   - Position: Supine, knees bent (hook-lying position), feet flat on the floor.
	   - Instructions: 
	     - Begin with the drawing-in maneuver as described above.
	     - While maintaining the abdominal contraction, slowly lift one foot off the floor to a 90-degree bend at the hip and knee.
	     - Hold for 2-3 seconds, then slowly lower the foot back to the floor.
	     - Repeat on the other side.
	     - Perform 10-15 repetitions per side, ensuring the pelvis remains stable throughout the movement.

	Next Position: Quadruped (Hands and Knees)

	3. Exercise in Quadruped: Bird-Dog
	   - Position: On hands and knees (quadruped position), with hands directly under shoulders and knees under hips.
	   - Instructions: 
	     - Start in the quadruped position, maintaining a neutral spine.
	     - Perform the drawing-in maneuver to engage the transverse abdominis.
	     - Slowly extend one leg straight back while simultaneously extending the opposite arm forward, keeping both parallel to the floor.
	     - Hold for 5-10 seconds, maintaining balance and a stable core.
	     - Return to the starting position and switch sides.
	     - Perform 10 repetitions per side.

	4. Modifications to Adjust Difficulty:

	   - To Make the Exercise Less Challenging:
	     - Perform the exercise by extending only the leg or only the arm, rather than both simultaneously. This reduces the stability challenge.
	     - Shorten the hold time to 2-3 seconds instead of 5-10 seconds, reducing the duration of maintaining balance and stability.

	   - To Make the Exercise More Challenging:
	     - Increase the hold time to 10-15 seconds to enhance endurance and stability.
	     - Add a resistance band around the thighs for added resistance during the leg lift, increasing the difficulty of maintaining stability.

	Summary

This is advanced within the same position by incorporating single-leg marches to add a dynamic component while maintaining stability. Adjustments in difficulty can be made by modifying the hold time or adding resistance to suit the patient's abilities and progression.

3.19. Advanced Abdominal Training
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

●	Advanced Abdominal Training
	-Tailoring exercises to the functional demands of the patient while avoiding unnecessary exacerbation of symptoms.
	-Some patients can be challenged to a significant degree while performing stabilization exercises.  However, as previously mentioned, exacerbations after low back injuries are common and it is easy to prescribe exercises that are too challenging that result in increased pain and set the patient’s progress back.
	-Stabilization exercises are meant to provide overload but with the following principle in mind:
		-“The objective is to challenge muscle systems to achieve sufficient functional stability but in a way that spares the spine of excessive exacerbating load.”

Easy Ways to cause excessive load
	-When prescribing challenging exercises, be wary of these potential issues associated with the manner in which the patient is performing the exercise or the nature of the exercise itself.
		■	Use long lever arms to increase load
			●	Can result in excessive torque and sheer forces on the spine
		■	Rotational movements under load
			●	Excess torque and sheer forces on the spine
		■	Hyperextension
			●	Typically avoided altogether but can cause compression of facet joints
		■	Exercising after excess fatigue
			●	Can result in the patient being unable to maintain proper spinal position and moving into harmful positions or ranges

3.21. Integration of Function
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

-Integration of function is considered to be the final progression. 
-Incorporating kinesthetic awareness principles into the patient’s functional activities. 
-As soon as is feasible, the physical therapist should engage in activities that help the principles of kinesthetic awareness integrate into their everyday activities.
-Spinal stabilization during function involves the following kinesthetic and training principles:
	Teaching the patient to maintain proper spine position during ADL’s and other functional activities.
		First statically for protection, progressing to movement within expanded ranges.
	Efficiently recruit trunk musculature during these activities.
	Move through full but still safe spinal range during function.
		-Again, this typically involves avoiding the extremes of motion and understanding circumstances (increased load or demand) where this can easily occur.
-Training the patient to progress to movement through a “functional range” can be difficult.

	Acute pain vs. Chronic (Persistent) Pain
		Acute:
			Short in duration (typically last no more than 3-6 months)
			Typically resolves with healing
			Pain is generally caused by an event that causes damage to soft tissue
			Serves as a warning to prevent further damage
		Chronic:
			Persists beyond normal healing time of an injury (> 6 months)
			May not be directly associated with injury or illness
			Outlasts its usefulness
	Nociceptive (Active Pain) vs. Centrally Generated Pain
		Nociceptive Pain: pain predominantly driven by activation of peripheral nociceptive sensory fibers
			Localized near area of injury
			Clear aggravating/easing factors
			Intermittent and sharp with movement or constant dull ache at rest
		Central Sensitization: Amplification of neural signaling in the CNS that elicits pain hypersensitivity
			Non-mechanical, unpredictable pain prevocational patterns
			Non-specific and multiple aggrevating/easing factors
			Painful areas are diffuse in non-anatomic patterns
			Disproportionate response to initiating stimuli
	Consideration of Psychological Factors in treatment of Chronic Pain
		Monikers for Clinical Approaches that Address Psychological in Addition to Physiological Factors:
			Pain neuroscience
			Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
			“Psychologically informed” therapy

	Video Follow-up Questions
		1.What does the video describe as the main difference of the source of acute pain vs. chronic (persistent) pain?
			(Γ)Acute pain typically arises from tissue damage, such as from a back injury or ankle sprain, and is common in the initial stages of injury. It is characterized by a sharp, dull, strong, or mild sensation, and individuals are usually encouraged to stay active and gradually resume normal activities, including work. On the other hand, chronic or persistent pain, which lasts for three months or more, is less associated with tissue damage. Instead, it is primarily caused by the brain continuing to produce pain signals even after the body tissues have healed and are no longer in danger. Thus, while acute pain is directly related to tissue damage, chronic pain is more about the sensitivity of the nervous system and ongoing pain signals produced by the brain.

4.6. Overview of Neurodynamics
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

Neurodynamics
	-Neurodynamics: a broad term that is defined as the study of the mechanics and physiology of the nervous system and their interrelations
	-Adverse neural tension:
		-When a patient experiences a pathology that affects the mechanics and physiology of the peripheral nervous system, this is known as "adverse neural tension"
		-More specifically, "adverse neural tension" is considered abnormal physiological and mechanical responses produced from nervous system structures when testing its normal movement and elastic capabilities

Aspects of Neurodynamics
	Mechanics of Peripheral Nerves
		-These represent the physical ability for peripheral nerve structures to exhibit properties of mobility and elasticity
		-These structural properties are important to maintain peripheral nerve integrity and function. Peripheral nerves course throughout the trunk and extremities and therefore, must be able to accommodate movements of these areas
		-As movements occur, nerves must be:
			Mobile enough to slide within their connective tissue sheaths
			Lengthen accordingly as the surrounding tissue is placed on stretch
	Physiology of Peripheral Nerves
		This refers to the biological and chemical processes that occur within peripheral nerves
		Examples include:
			-Blood flow to, from, and within peripheral nerves
			-Axoplasmic flow that maintains the structural component of individual neurons within peripheral nerves
			-Function of ion channels that lead to a change in action potential
			-Conduction of impulses along peripheral nerves that allow communication between central nervous system structures and the periphery

4.8. Classifications of Neurodynamics Pathomechanics
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

Classifications of neurodynamics pathomechanics
	Container Dependent Dysfunction
		A neurodynamic impairment created by interference with a “mechanical interface”
			-Examples include
				Stenosis
				Disc herniation
				Carpal tunnel syndrome
				Muscular & fascial entrapments
			-This type of impairment implies that the nerve is trapped inside some space or cavity through which it passes, preventing it from gliding or sliding during normal movement 
	Neural dependent dysfunction 
		A neurodynamic impairment due to a loss of a nerve’s mechanical properties
			Adherent nerve root
			Neural fibrosis
			Contracted nerve tissue
			Tethered (anchored/adherido/adherit/ancorat) nerve tissue
		-This type of impairment implies that the nerve has become less elastic and therefor cannot elongate as needed during movement 
	The presence of these neural impairments
		-Will result in an immediate decrease in nerve function due to the effects of compression or stretch
		-Will result in further physiological changes and functional decline overtime due to sustained and repetitive stresses

	Clinical note:
		-Nerves trapped in a container or with loss of mechanical properties will decrease in function quicker when under conditions of stretch or compression versus normal nerves.

4.9.2. Additional Considerations for Diagnostic Testing Part 2
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

Another variation of diagnostic testing is to apply tension using “slider” vs. “tensioner” techniques.  
	Slider Technique
		-Assesses ability of nerve to slide or glide naturally during motion.
		-This is achieved by producing tension in the distal component of the nerve while releasing tension in proximal component.
		-This affects is comparable to what occurs when one uses dental floss, pulling on one end of the floss while releasing the other end and then reversing the process (this is why some also call neurodynamics “neural flossing”).
		-For example, performance of a slider technique on the Med Nerve would involve.
			-Maintaining elbow ext & moving the wrist into extension (increasing distal tension) without depressing the shoulder (releasing proximal tension) followed by:
				-Moving the wrist into flexion (releasing distal tension) while depressing the shoulder (adding proximal tension).
		--------------------------------------------------------------------
		Try it Out!
			-You can perform an active variation of this on yourself by alternating neck and wrist movements. Try this now.
				1.Place your arm and neck in the position below (slight wrist extension, elbow extension and slight side bending of the neck away from the arm until you feel slight tingling in your arm or hand.
				2.Simultaneously side-bend your head TOWARDS the arm while slightly further extending the wrist. At first, you should feel the tingling diminish but then return as the wrist moves into extension.
				3.Then reverse the process. Flex wrist the wrist slightly to release distal tension while creating more proximal tension by side-bending the head away.
			('53 MEDIAN NERVE GLIDE - D -hep2go.mp4') 
	Tensioner Technique
		-Assesses the nerve’s ability to respond to mechanical elongation.
			-A tensioner is achieved via adding neural tension at both proximal and distal ends simultaneously.
		-Essentially, the physical therapist adds tension by moving one joint at a time towards the end range tension position until symptoms are produced.
		-An example of the tensioner technique is performing a basic straight leg raise.

4.10. Neurodynamics Interventions
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

-Finally, the point of assessing neurodynamics and identifying adverse neural tension is to provide interventions that will decrease this type of impairment.
-Slider and tensioner techniques are the two primary manual therapy approaches to providing neurodynamics interventions.
-Slider” vs. “Tensioner” Treatment (Rx) Techniques

-Review the following comparisons between slider vs. tensioner techniques for treatment.
	Slider Rx Technique
		-By producing tension in distal component while releasing tension in proximal component until symptoms first are perceived, followed by releasing distal tension and increasing proximal tension until symptoms are again first perceived
		-This procedure is repeated several times
		-Ideally, the patient will report feeling very slight tingling symptoms at either extreme of motion and a disappearing or reduction of symptoms in the middle of the range
		-Performance of a slider technique for the median nerve would include
			Slight tingling during wrist extension and side-bend towards the arm
			Diminished tingling at the beginning of wrist flexion and neck side-bending
			Return of slight tingling during additional wrist flexion and neck side bending
			Repeat
	Tensioner Rx Technique
		-A tensioner is performed as a treatment described previously but once symptoms are reproduced the therapist produces oscillations of one joint in a manner that alternatively, but very slight increases and decreases tension
		-For the median nerve this could be accomplished by oscillating:
			The wrist in and out of extension
			The elbow in and out of extension
			Depressing and releasing depression of the shoulder
		-It should be emphasized that the perception of increases in tension should be very slight
		-A good cue to the patient is that during each movement you want them to feel “pressure on, pressure off, pressure on, pressure off…”

('PT 2232_Mod 4_Slider_Tensioner Treatments-1.pdf')
	”Slider” vs. “Tensioner” Treatment Techniques 
	When to use which? 
	 In General:  
		-A tensioner technique is more appropriate for nerve tissue remodeling – that is, treatment of a 
		neural dependent condition.  
			-This is because the tensioner will help the nerve regain elastic properties. 
		-A slider technique is often more appropriate for treatment of a container dysfunction. 
			-This is to help the nerve glide and slide more efficiently through the anatomical space in which it has become trapped or adhered. 
		-In the absence of obvious distinction between slider and tensioner techniques, attempt each and 
		determine which most effectively reduces the patient’s symptoms. 
		-As a general rule – Overall, PTs are more likely to use neurodynamics treatments with neural 
		dependent conditions than container dependent conditions. 
		-For container dysfunction, you may first need to first address mobility or flexibility of the container itself (then later address nerve mobility).   
			For example: 
				Spinal containers (neural foramina, bony spurs) 
					Identify (ID) directional preference or position of unloading and mobilize the nerve in these positions.  
		-Articular containers 
			Restore joint play and motion prior to performing neurodynamics treatments. 
		-Contractile containers 
			Restore length of muscle/tendon complexes first. 

	Basic Treatment Principles  
		1. The intensity of maneuver should be inversely related to irritability of tissue. 
		2. The beginning point of treatment should be at the first perception of adverse neurodynamics such as: 
			a. Symptoms reported 
			b. OR - Tissue resistance identified by therapist 
		3. Oscillations of “pressure on, pressure off” performed into and out of range of adverse neurodynamics:  
			a. Reassess after ~ ten repetitions 
			b. Wait “minutes” or until symptoms subside 
			c. Repeat or not based on patient status 
			d. Eventually may work up to performing oscillations for 30-60 seconds at a time 
			e. With neurodynamics, always err on the side of more conservative treatment 
		4. Provide self-treatment options if successful.

									Absolute Contraindications

										- Acute joint inflammation or infection
										- Malignancy involving the joint/spine
										- Severe vascular deficiency or impairment that could be worsened by mobilization
									Relative Contraindications
										- Severe osteopenia that increases fracture risk
										- Acute joint injury or pain that could be exacerbated 
										- Severe muscle spasm or guarding that prevents adequate relaxation
										- Neurological signs like numbness/weakness that could worsen with mobilization
										- Patient apprehension or inability to relax during the technique
									The main concerns are avoiding techniques that could further injure compromised bony or soft tissues, provoke an inflammatory response, compromise vascular supply, or cause instability. Careful evaluation and clinical reasoning are required, as many contraindications are relative based on the severity and irritability of the condition. Proper patient screening, handling, and force application are critical for safe joint mobilization.
